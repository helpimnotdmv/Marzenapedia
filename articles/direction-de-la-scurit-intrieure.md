# Direction de la Sécurité Intérieure

The **Direction de la Sécurité Intérieure** (**DSI**) is the domestic intelligence and national-security police service of the [[Republic of Marzena]]. It is responsible for protecting the constitutional order, countering espionage and foreign interference, preventing terrorism and organised political violence, defending strategic industries, auditing the security implications of foreign investment, administering national security clearances, protecting the President and Prime Minister, and coordinating domestic technical counter-intelligence and cyber defence. Unlike the [[Direction du Renseignement Extérieur et Technique|DRET]], which has no ordinary police jurisdiction inside Marzena, the DSI possesses specialised powers of surveillance, search, arrest and evidentiary investigation for a defined category of national-security offences.

The DSI is legally a service of the **Ministère de l'Intérieur**, but it is not a directorate of the [[Police Nationale Marzenienne]] (PNM). Its Director-General is appointed directly by the President, its strategic priorities are set within the presidential national-security domain, and its complete reporting is distributed to the permanent members of the [[Conseil National de Sécurité]]. The Interior Minister is formally responsible for its legality, administration and budget. The Prime Minister receives national-security reporting and is protected by the service, but possesses little independent authority to redirect its operations. This arrangement makes the DSI characteristic of Marzenian executive government: ministerial in law, presidential in strategic orientation and professionally self-governing in daily practice.

The service describes itself as an **integrated national-security police**, concentrating intelligence, protective security, disruption and arrest without acquiring general police authority. Homicide, organised crime, narcotics, financial crime and local street intelligence belong to the **Direction Centrale de la Police Judiciaire** (DCPJ). DSI personnel do not share its brigade identity or neck-badge tradition; overt teams use DSI armbands or jackets, while covert officers may remain unidentified until arrest.

The contemporary structure emerged from a long effort to distinguish state security from political policing and national intelligence from criminal investigation. Centralised in 1981, weakened by the transfer of urban intelligence to the police **Direction Centrale du Renseignement Intérieur** (DCRI) in 1989, and divided again by the joint **Section de Contre-Terrorisme et des Affaires Spéciales** (SCTAS), it accumulated overlapping claims for decades. In 2024 both rivals were abolished, leaving the DSI with national-security responsibility and the DCPJ with a pure criminal-investigation mandate.

The settlement is regarded as successful professionalisation, but it did not resolve the central controversy. The DSI protects individual citizenship, secularism and legal equality, yet those principles remain culturally associated with the coastal, francophone class that built the republic. Its doctrine insists that belief alone can never justify investigation. Critics answer that ideological danger and cultural nonconformity are still distinguished by officials whose identity is treated as neutral.

## History

### Colonial antecedents and establishment

The DSI's institutional lineage derives from the political-security and counter-espionage elements of the colonial **Sûreté Générale**. Those sections maintained political files, monitored anticolonial organisations, cultivated informants in ports and railway districts, and supported French counter-intelligence against Axis, Spanish and later Soviet-linked activity in the western Mediterranean. Their function was colonial preservation rather than republican security. Their techniques—central personal files, source registers, postal interception, association mapping and the classification of political networks by assessed capability—nonetheless provided the administrative foundation inherited by the independent state.

On 2 May 1952 the colonial Sûreté was formally dissolved. Its uniformed and criminal branches were reconstituted as the Police Nationale, while the state-security sections became the **Direction de la Sécurité Intérieure**. The decision reflected more than administrative convenience. The founders of the republic considered state security a national and indivisible function that could not be distributed among municipal or territorial police commands. They also believed that the new state would face two kinds of immediate challenge: foreign penetration of the independence settlement and organised resistance to the constitutional order from groups that rejected its secular, unitary and francophone foundations.

The early service changed personnel more rapidly than technique. French advisers remained during the 1950s, inherited files were recatalogued, and surveillance practices acquired republican authorisations without an immediate methodological break. Official history calls this the nationalisation of usable capacity; critics call it the conversion of colonial political knowledge into the property of the coastal state.

### Frontier war and constitutional consolidation

The [[La Guerre des Frontières (1963)|frontier war of 1963]] made Algerian counter-espionage the DSI's defining professional mission. The conflict revealed the scale of cross-border collection, political cultivation and logistical preparation occurring inside Marzena. In response, the DSI built permanent border-counterintelligence desks, expanded liaison with ports and strategic employers, and established the first central system for comparing foreign contacts, unexplained financial flows and access to sensitive facilities. Algerian services became the reference adversary against which generations of DSI officers learned their profession.

The political crisis with the Arab League and the constitutional revision of 1964 broadened that counter-espionage logic. The revision entrenched the republican form, *laïcité*, the unitary state and French as the language of the Republic within the constitutional order. It prohibited religious and ethnic parties and strengthened state control over foreign-supported religious endowments. Protection of those principles became an express DSI mission. Foreign interference and domestic constitutional opposition could consequently appear in the same file: external pressure operated through local religious, linguistic and regional networks, while many such networks pursued lawful grievances that could not coherently be reduced to foreign direction.

Headquarters distinguished legitimate grievance, patronage networks, regional mobilisation, clandestine separatism and foreign-supported coercion. Field reporting was often less careful. Berber cultural associations complained that linguistic claims were recorded beside security threats because the constitutional system lacked a recognised category for communal political demands. This gap between analytic doctrine and field practice became a recurring pattern.

### The 1971 overlap

The Police Nationale reorganisation of 1971 created the **Direction Centrale des Renseignements Généraux** (DCRG) as a police political-intelligence directorate. The DCRG maintained local sources, monitored public order and reported on organisations active in the major cities. It did not remove the DSI's authority over counter-espionage, constitutional security or nationally organised violence. Instead it introduced a second route through which political information reached the Interior Ministry.

The overlap initially appeared manageable. The DCRG knew neighbourhoods, associations, labour disputes and local organisers; the DSI knew foreign services, strategic institutions and national networks. In practice, the same person could be a local political activist, the associate of an Algerian officer and a participant in a violent organisation. Each service tended to record the part of the relationship fitting its own mandate. Neither possessed a complete picture, and source ownership became a recurring cause of non-disclosure.

The weakness was exposed in the **Fernant affair of 1980**, involving a failed sequence of politically motivated attacks. Police intelligence knew the local militants and their warehouse; the DSI tracked an Algerian-linked intermediary and unexplained transfers; the gendarmerie had recorded weapons movement. No institution assembled the streams before a fire exposed the explosives. The scandal was that the state possessed almost every fact without producing shared knowledge.

### The 1981 unification

The response was the **Loi organique relative à la sécurité intérieure of 1981**. Its principal architect was a senior Interior Ministry technocrat supported by the Presidency rather than a political officeholder publicly identified with the reform. The law unified national counter-espionage, counter-terrorism, constitutional protection and central threat analysis within the DSI. It also established a common source registry for national-security cases and formalised the service's authority to request police and gendarmerie assistance.

The reform's maxim was ***Unité de connaissance, légalité d'action***—unity of knowledge, legality of action. The first clause expressed the lesson of Fernant: the state could not permit institutional ownership to prevent synthesis of information about the same threat. The second rejected the proposition that intelligence unity should erase legal responsibility. Surveillance, search, arrest and prosecution were to remain attributable to an authorised official under an identifiable statute, even when the underlying assessment combined secret material from several institutions.

The settlement created national mission directorates, regional bureaux and a distinction between intelligence officers and sworn investigators. It also concentrated threat intelligence and physical protection of the President and Prime Minister. Less openly, centralisation prevented the growing fragmentation of the PRNL and competitive politics from dividing national-security knowledge. The DSI was built to remain continuous when political authority no longer did.

### Democratic reform and the DCRI

The constitutional and police reforms of 1989 challenged that settlement. Reformers accepted the need for national counter-espionage and security analysis but distrusted the DSI's reach into urban politics. They sought to place political and community intelligence inside an ordinary police structure subject to clearer judicial procedure and visible ministerial accountability. The DCRG was accordingly consolidated as the **Direction Centrale du Renseignement Intérieur** (DCRI), and substantial parts of the DSI's urban source and surveillance capacity were transferred to it.

The transfer significantly weakened the DSI. It retained national authority but lost direct access to many of the sources through which national networks became visible. The DCRI, meanwhile, possessed local access without full responsibility for the threat that information might reveal. Memoranda attempted to distinguish urban political intelligence from national security, but the distinction rarely survived operational contact. Both services opened files on the same individuals, competed for sources and disputed which institution could authorise action. The DCRI became, in retrospect, a failed attempt to subordinate domestic intelligence to ordinary police command without abolishing the national service whose existence made such subordination incomplete.

### SCTAS and the problem of coercive ownership

The emergence of JWH and other networked security threats created a further problem. Marzena possessed capable criminal investigators and specialised police intervention teams, but no single national tactical police unit owned the progression from domestic intelligence to a terrorism arrest. In 2009 the government created the **Section de Contre-Terrorisme et des Affaires Spéciales** (SCTAS), formally within the DCPJ but staffed and financed jointly with the DSI.

SCTAS combined DCPJ evidentiary practice, selected tactical personnel and DSI intelligence access, and achieved several important arrests. It never acquired an agreed identity. DCPJ personnel called themselves “the cops,” responsible for proof, charge and the case file; DSI personnel were “the spooks,” concerned with sources, threat networks and the danger of premature prosecution. Both claimed control. Joint signatures and rotating commanders made responsibility less rather than more legible, while long-serving SCTAS officers developed a hybrid culture but weak career prospects in either parent institution.

### The 2024 settlement

The post-2022 escalation of the [[Interior Insurgency]] accelerated a reform that had already been discussed as administrative rationalisation. DCRI coverage increasingly duplicated DSI files, while SCTAS required senior arbitration for operations that the DSI was already assessing and the DCPJ no longer wished to own. The government presented abolition as part of a broader efficiency programme rather than as an emergency response. That framing made the reform politically easier: the DCRI had become sufficiently redundant that its disappearance created no significant capability gap.

The **Loi organique portant réorganisation des services de sécurité intérieure**, adopted in 2024 after intense Assembly debate, abolished the DCRI and SCTAS completely. National-security officers from the DCRI transferred selectively to the DSI; officers whose expertise concerned crime, local informants or evidentiary investigation transferred to the DCPJ. DSI secondees in SCTAS returned to the service, while DCPJ personnel returned to criminal brigades. The DSI inherited the DCRI archive and conducted an internal review of its files, but the archive was not placed under an external destruction process. This decision remains one of the reform's principal civil-liberties controversies.

The law did not convert the DSI into a general detective service. It instead gave the service complete intelligence-to-arrest responsibility for terrorism, espionage and organised national-security violence. The DCPJ was stripped of a separate counter-terrorism jurisdiction and defined more clearly as the national criminal-investigation directorate. Case-specific cooperation continues where homicide, financial crime or organised crime intersects national security, but the permanent hybrid institution disappeared.

All major parties accepted that duplication had failed. Supporters argued that clear authority made operations more lawful because responsibility could finally be identified. Opponents observed that one institution could now generate a secret assessment, select a disruption, conduct surveillance, make an arrest and shape the information available for challenge. The reform professionalised domestic security by concentrating responsibility; it also concentrated its risks.

## Legal status and governance

### A ministerial service with a presidential director

The DSI is a statutory national-security police service under the **Ministère de l'Intérieur**. Its legal personality, coercive powers and enumerated offences are established by organic law rather than by administrative convention. It is organisationally separate from the Police Nationale and from the DRET. Regional DSI directors report to Lévane, not to local police chiefs or prefects, although prefects may receive threat briefings and request security assessments.

The service's constitutional position is deliberately asymmetrical. The **President** appoints the Director-General directly, may remove the holder at executive confidence and sets general national-security priorities. In formal practice, removal is recorded through the Council of Ministers and reasons are supplied privately to the parliamentary intelligence committee; in political reality, a Director-General who loses presidential confidence does not remain in office. Appointment has always been made from within the professional DSI corps, normally selecting a senior officer with legal or administrative formation. The absence of a fixed term makes the office responsive to the Presidency while the professional appointment tradition limits overt partisan selection.

The **Interior Minister** exercises statutory responsibility. The minister presents the budget, issues administrative regulations, answers to Parliament for the service and receives notice of exceptionally sensitive operations. The office is institutionally important but informationally dependent: only the Director-General possesses a complete view of current operations, sources and technical limitations. Ministers able to question DSI assessments effectively have normally relied on a small cleared cabinet rather than the ministry's ordinary directorates.

The **Prime Minister** receives full reporting as a permanent CNS member but cannot normally initiate or redirect an investigation. Under cohabitation, the office may influence resources, legislation and ministerial cooperation but cannot replace DSI leadership or countermand a lawful presidential priority. The President, in turn, may set threat priorities but not lawfully order investigation of a named opponent or decide an individual case.

### National Security Council and intelligence product

The Director-General is the principal domestic-security adviser to the **Conseil National de Sécurité** (CNS) and a permanent institutional participant in its meetings. Full DSI reporting is available to all permanent members, including the President, Prime Minister, Interior Minister, Defence Minister, CEMA and principal intelligence directors. Distribution outside that group is tailored to institutional need. A strategic-industry minister may receive a foreign-investment assessment; a prefect may receive a regional threat picture; neither receives the source reporting or unrelated operational context behind it.

The service produces **threat assessments**, **protective assessments** and **operational dossiers**. The last support surveillance, disruption or arrest and are held to a different evidentiary standard from strategic analysis. A central analytical directorate prevents an operational team's assumptions from becoming the service's national judgement without challenge.

### Budget and oversight architecture

The DSI is funded through a published appropriation and classified operational annex. The latter covers sources, sensitive technology, protected transport, covert premises and designated investigations. Detailed programme totals reach a restricted joint committee covering the DSI and DRET, while operational transactions and live-source identities remain withheld.

The committee may inspect legal authorities, aggregate operations, serious force incidents, selected closed files and expenditure categories. It questions a Director-General nominee in restricted session without confirming the appointment. Published summaries are general, but classified review has changed retention practice and challenged programme renewals.

The **Inspection générale de la sécurité intérieure** (IGSI) operates outside the operational chain. It investigates misconduct, unlawful access, source violations and noncompliance with judicial conditions, and may report directly to the parliamentary committee and prosecutor. Its formal independence exceeds ordinary police inspection, though its personnel come from the same narrow professional milieu.

## Mandate

### Protection of the constitutional order

The DSI's first statutory responsibility is protection of the **constitutional, secular, democratic and unitary order** against organised threats. This includes institutions as well as principles: violence against the Assembly, coercive infiltration of a ministry or conspiracy to obstruct an election falls within the mandate even without a systematic ideology.

An ideological threat is legally defined through capability and conduct rather than belief. The relevant indicators are organised preparation to replace constitutional principles through violence, coercion, clandestine foreign support or the construction of parallel authority. Public rejection of *laïcité*, advocacy of constitutional revision or support for territorial autonomy does not by itself satisfy the threshold. The DSI may open an assessment where there is documented evidence of covert support, organisational capability or connection to coercive activity. Membership in a lawful organisation cannot alone justify an individual security file.

This distinction structures treatment of the **Alliance Nationale Conservatrice**. Party membership is not a security indicator, but the DSI examines traditionalist networks where Gulf-associated finance, concealed direction or proximity to radicalisation creates an independently documented concern. Supporters regard the differentiation as professional restraint; the ANC argues that isolating a “network” from the party around it lets political monitoring continue under another name.

Regionalist politics is treated similarly. Autonomy, linguistic recognition and constitutional change are lawful; armed linkage, foreign direction and coercive parallel institutions are not. DSI analysis of the **Mouvement de Résistance des Plateaux** therefore separates political leaders, clandestine logistical actors and limited armed contacts, although investigating one inevitably reveals information about the others.

The service examines demonstrations only for defined threats to officials, institutions or organised violence; public order remains with police or gendarmerie. Online it maps extremist ecosystems and specific covert networks but denies maintaining general ideological files. Its inheritance of the complete DCRI archive complicates that assurance.

The most controversial constitutional-protection power is not arrest but **administrative exclusion**. A DSI assessment may prevent a person from receiving a security clearance, entering a protected site or occupying sensitive public employment without establishing a criminal offence. The person may challenge a clearance decision through classified administrative procedure, but the underlying sources and methods may remain concealed. The system therefore protects institutions against risks that cannot be proved publicly by imposing consequences that are formally preventive rather than penal.

### Counter-espionage and anti-interference

The DSI detects, surveils, disrupts and arrests foreign intelligence activity within Marzenian jurisdiction. It is also the principal counter-industrial-espionage service, reflecting the strategic importance of defence, nuclear engineering, aerospace, telecommunications and sovereign digital systems. Espionage investigations frequently combine human sources, access records, corporate ownership analysis, financial tracing and technical support obtained from the DRET.

Algeria remains the service's historical and operational reference adversary. Algerian networks have used diplomatic personnel, commercial intermediaries, diaspora relationships and southern trade routes for collection and influence. The DSI's professional vocabulary—agent access, logistical preparation, cultivated intermediary and institutional penetration—was built around that contest. Chinese economic and technical collection has risen in importance, particularly around electronics, data infrastructure and the defence-industrial supply chain, but has not displaced Algeria as the principal state counter-intelligence target.

Friendship affects the response to espionage, not its classification. Allied activity is more likely to result in surveillance, private warning or expulsion than prosecution. French commercial and institutional networks are both indispensable and capable of creating influence channels that would be treated more aggressively if associated with an adversary.

Foreign interference is defined more broadly than espionage. It includes covert political funding, concealed direction of associations, disinformation undertaken on behalf of a state, intimidation of diaspora communities and clandestine attempts to shape public decisions. Community-facing policing remains outside the DSI unless a named security concern emerges. Ordinary religious, linguistic or expatriate association is not grounds for standing coverage. Once a foreign-directed network is identified, the DSI may investigate its domestic members while the DRET collects against the sponsoring structure abroad.

The DSI controls arrests inside Marzena and coordinates with the DRET without surrendering police command. Double-agent operations require Director-General approval and notification to a specialised security magistrate. Its principal weakness is technical dependence: DRET personnel are sometimes “leased” to domestic operations under DSI authority, giving the service capabilities it could not sustain alone while creating a possible workaround when domestic technical procedure is slower or more restrictive.

### Counter-terrorism and urban neutralisation

The DSI possesses full intelligence-to-arrest responsibility for terrorism and organised national-security violence. It collects sources, develops threat assessments, conducts authorised surveillance, chooses between prosecution and preventive disruption, and executes high-risk arrests through its own intervention capability. The service does not require proof of a completed offence before beginning an intelligence investigation. Coercive actions must nevertheless rest on a statutory authority distinct from the analytic judgement that produced the target.

The internal term ***neutralisation urbaine*** refers primarily to locating and safely arresting armed cells before they can act. It includes surveillance, containment, communications denial, evacuation, negotiated surrender and tactical entry. It does not formally mean assassination or disappearance. The term became public through leaked training material and remains controversial because “neutralisation” appears to describe the removal of a threat rather than the enforcement of law. The DSI has retained it internally while using “preventive intervention” in public communications.

**Jaysh al-Watan al-Haqiqi** (JWH) is the service's highest-priority target. The DSI treats JWH as the domestic branch of a transnational Islamist movement rather than as a purely indigenous organisation or a directly commanded foreign franchise. Its operational decisions are local, but training, ideological material, movement and logistical support connect it to a wider Sahel environment. This interpretation gives the DSI domestic case authority while making DRET reporting indispensable.

The service distinguishes JWH from the MRP and **Réseau Sud**. The MRP contains lawful political advocacy, clandestine logistics and limited insurgent activity; Réseau Sud is a protection economy rather than a coherent ideological enemy. JWH may exploit both without controlling either.

Terrorism finance is handled inside the DSI by the economic-security and financial-investigation elements of the counter-terrorism directorate. The service may draw upon DCPJ forensic accountants and Finance Ministry records, but it owns the national-security investigation. Ambiguous mass violence remains with ordinary criminal police until ideological or organised national-security intent is established. This rule prevents the DSI from acquiring every politically resonant crime while allowing a case to transfer when motive becomes clear.

Preventive powers include surveillance, temporary financial holds, travel restrictions, foreign-agent registration, clearance revocation and exclusion from protected facilities. Penetration of extremist organisations requires prior judicial approval. The post-2022 campaign has reinforced the conclusion that urban recruitment, rural combat, foreign support, prison organisation and informal trade cannot be understood as separate theatres.

### Economic defence and foreign investment

Economic defence protects strategic sectors, technologies, supply chains and decision-making against espionage, sabotage, concealed control and coercive dependence. Designated sectors include defence, energy, ports, telecommunications and data, aerospace, nuclear infrastructure, critical finance and the sovereign digital systems supporting government. The DSI possesses inspection powers but does not maintain permanent officers inside private firms. Cleared corporate-security officials report significant incidents and respond to targeted inquiries.

Under the **Loi sur les investissements étrangers dans les secteurs stratégiques**, above-threshold transactions and any acquisition involving a designated strategic asset receive review. The DSI investigates ownership, financing, foreign-state relationships and dependencies embedded in licensing or maintenance contracts. Its recommendation is influential but not binding; the Council of Ministers decides.

Security conditions short of prohibition are negotiated by the Finance Ministry after the DSI identifies the risk. These may include data localisation, security segregation, replacement of suppliers, Marzenian representation on a board or limits on voting control. The division allows the DSI to state the security problem without becoming the commercial negotiator. Challenges to an adverse decision normally proceed through administrative law and focus on procedural legality; the courts have been reluctant to substitute their own judgement for a classified national-security assessment.

[[AXIOME Systèmes]] and [[Groupe Karmarena]] are indispensable strategic partners but remain subject to insider-threat and foreign-dependence review. Critics contend that proximity makes intrusive investigation of establishment firms less likely than scrutiny of foreign or interior-associated capital. More broadly, secret national-security reasoning can conceal protectionism: the public sees the decision but not enough of the assessment to distinguish sovereign caution from protection of incumbent interests.

### Personnel security and clearances

The DSI administers security clearance for officials, military and intelligence personnel, strategic-industry employees and contractors requiring classified access. The DSI decision is binding: an employing institution may deny access on additional grounds but may not grant classified access after a DSI refusal. Vetting examines foreign leverage, concealed financial vulnerability, serious criminal conduct, unauthorised disclosure and demonstrated organisational links to extremist or foreign-directed activity.

Political belief alone is not a lawful factor. Concealed commitment to an organisation, willingness to violate constitutional duties or an undisclosed relationship creating foreign leverage may be considered. No religious test exists. Journalists, rejected applicants and some former officers nevertheless allege an informal preference for candidates who display the secular cultural manner of the senior state even where observance has no connection to risk.

Higher clearances add personal interviews and examination of unexplained access patterns. Continuous vetting is limited to the highest levels and formally concerns security-relevant anomalies rather than general lifestyle monitoring. Refusal may be challenged before a classified administrative panel, which supplies meaningful but incomplete reasons. The central controversy is **professional alignment**: candidates resembling the existing institutional class are more readily understood as predictable, while unfamiliar associations require explanation.

### Executive protection

The DSI has protected the President and Prime Minister since 1981. It controls the complete protective environment: close-protection teams, residences, secure transport, route planning, advance reconnaissance, threat assessment, communications, evacuation and coordination of local police and gendarmerie support. Marzena maintains no separate military presidential guard. Ceremonial security and site presentation are performed within the DSI protection system rather than by a politically distinct household force.

The President receives the larger permanent apparatus. Prime-ministerial protection is smaller and partly reinforced by ordinary police for public events, reflecting the unequal strategic status of the offices. Major presidential candidates may receive temporary DSI protection after an official threat assessment. Visiting foreign leaders are protected under DSI command with territorial police and gendarmerie assistance.

Protective teams are prohibited from collecting political intelligence unrelated to safety, and their records are compartmented. Rules cannot eliminate the underlying concern: the service preserving executive continuity observes the people exercising it more closely than almost any other institution.

### Technical counter-intelligence and cyber defence

The DSI coordinates general domestic cyber defence and investigates national-security cyber activity. Its technical directorate detects covert transmitters, compromised devices, hostile network access, clandestine surveillance and penetration of protected facilities. It leads incident coordination across civilian government and designated strategic operators, attributes domestic access where possible and supports the disruption of active threats.

This broad mandate does not imply technical self-sufficiency. The directorate is small and substantially dependent on the DRET for advanced signals collection, cryptanalysis, foreign infrastructure access and specialised personnel. Domestic communications interception normally occurs through DRET collection systems operated under DSI legal authority. Where an external cyber action is necessary to terminate an imminent threat inside Marzena, the DSI may authorise the domestic-security objective, but the DRET normally executes the foreign technical act.

Targeted interception and technical surveillance require specialised judicial authorisation. In an urgent national-security case the Director-General may authorise temporary intrusive cyber action subject to prompt judicial review. Metadata collection is targeted and network-based, permitting limited expansion from authorised selectors rather than unrestricted bulk access. Retrospective facial recognition may be used against stored footage; continuous real-time identification of the general public is not an acknowledged DSI capability.

DSI access to the **Système National d'Identité Marzenienne** (MNIS) is role-limited and audited under classified operating agreements. AXIOME maintains parts of the supporting infrastructure but cannot initiate an access request or view operational queries without a logged, supervised technical intervention. Algorithmic tools assist linkage analysis and triage but cannot alone justify arrest, exclusion or denial of clearance.

The technical problem is excess information and uneven analytic capacity. Authorisation may arrive too slowly while existing stores contain more data than the service can examine. DRET support partly solves and partly evades the difficulty: foreign collection can return information with domestic consequence through a system not built around domestic procedure.

## Police powers and operational procedure

### Specialised judicial-police status

Approximately forty to forty-five per cent of DSI personnel are sworn officers or investigators possessing **specialised national judicial-police status**. Their authority is limited to enumerated security offences, including espionage, terrorism, foreign interference, attacks on constitutional institutions, unlawful disclosure of protected strategic information and specified forms of sabotage. They do not acquire general responsibility for homicide, narcotics, ordinary financial crime or local public order merely because those offences may interest the state.

National-security arrests may be authorised internally where statutory grounds exist. This does not remove judicial control over intrusive measures. A planned operation involving search of a home, covert entry or communications interception requires the appropriate specialised warrant. Temporary arrest without prior judicial order is available where delay creates an immediate risk of violence, escape or destruction of critical evidence. The operational director authorises a planned raid under the applicable judicial authority; exceptionally sensitive actions are notified to the Director-General and political leadership without transferring tactical command.

The DSI operates registered secure police custody suites, and the ordinary pre-charge limit applies. Longer confinement requires a separate statutory mechanism, including judicial remand or the controversial CIS framework; it cannot be created by retaining a person in DSI custody.

Interrogation is conducted by specially certified investigators. Intelligence officers may advise on the network, contradictions and source sensitivities but do not lead an evidentiary interview. Where prosecution is intended, a separate evidentiary team reconstructs the case through lawful searches, witness testimony, financial records and technical material capable of disclosure or protected judicial handling. Intelligence may identify the target without itself becoming the evidence presented in open court.

### GISI

The **Groupe d'Intervention de la Sécurité Intérieure** (**GISI**) is the DSI's national tactical unit for armed terrorism, counter-espionage arrests and recovery of protected material. It has an estimated establishment of 180–240 operators and specialist support personnel. GISI includes assault, observation, explosive-entry, protective extraction, tactical medical and technical-search elements. It is compact enough to remain a specialised national capability rather than a parallel public-order force.

GISI places arrest at the centre of an operation but accepts a more anticipatory force threshold than ordinary policing. Where specific intelligence establishes that a suspect is armed or preparing immediate violence, officers need not wait for the threat to be displayed in the same manner expected during an ordinary arrest. The DSI calls this necessity applied to better information; critics call it force justified by evidence the public cannot test.

During overt action, GISI and other DSI officers use marked jackets, armbands and institutional identification. Covert teams may remain unidentified during approach and surveillance, revealing authority when intervention begins. The DCPJ neck badge is never authorised within the DSI. The distinction is cultural as well as visual: DCPJ investigators identify with the brigade and the case, while DSI operators identify with a protected mission and national chain of authority.

### Disruption and prosecution

The DSI may choose legally enumerated disruption where prosecution would expose a source, reveal a foreign collection capability or fail to prevent an imminent threat. Available measures include travel restriction, temporary financial hold, access revocation, foreign-agent registration and exclusion from protected facilities. A short emergency asset hold may be issued directly; continuation requires judicial or ministerial confirmation.

Undercover activity is subject to continuous prosecutorial control, and informant penetration of extremist organisations requires prior judicial approval. These requirements were strengthened during the 2024 reform as the political price of concentrating arrest power. They also generate operational frustration: officers argue that source recruitment cannot always be paused while a magistrate is briefed, particularly where the potential source approaches unexpectedly. Emergency protocols exist but require written justification and rapid review.

The DSI's jurisdiction extends throughout the Republic, including the Canary Islands and territories ordinarily policed by the gendarmerie. National jurisdiction does not eliminate practical dependence on territorial institutions. Local police know neighbourhoods, the gendarmerie knows interior routes, and neither body is placed permanently under DSI command. The service's unification is therefore unity of national-security responsibility, not ownership of every officer needed to exercise it.

| Authority | Scope | Normal control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intelligence assessment | Organised constitutional threats, espionage, terrorism and strategic-security risks | Internal threshold review; no coercive act follows automatically |

## Organisation

The post-2024 DSI is organised around eight national mission directorates, supported by regional bureaux, the IGSI and central administrative, legal and training offices. The directorates are functional rather than territorial. A regional office may contain officers assigned to several directorates, but the responsible national director controls doctrine, sensitive sources and major operational approval.

| Directorate | Abbreviation | Principal responsibilities |

| Direction de la Protection Exécutive | DPE | Protection of the President, Prime Minister, candidates and visiting leaders; secure transport and continuity planning |

### Operational arrangement

The **DLCT** is organised as a single intelligence-and-operations directorate rather than separate analytic and enforcement services. It contains distinct assessment, source, surveillance, financial and arrest divisions, with GISI available under central operational authority. Separation within the directorate is intended to preserve specialist practice without recreating the institutional ownership dispute that destroyed SCTAS.

The **DDEIS** combines economic defence and investment analysis, while Finance retains commercial negotiation. The **DSPH** is insulated from operational teams so that an officer pursuing a target cannot determine associated clearance cases. The **DAS** may issue a dissenting assessment where an operational judgement appears analytically self-confirming.

The **DTCI** is small relative to its mandate. Rather than reproduce the DRET's technical estate, it owns domestic legal compliance, protected-government coordination, technical searches and cyber incident command. Advanced collection teams may include DRET personnel temporarily assigned to a DSI case. This dependency is an acknowledged design choice and a recurrent source of institutional discomfort.

The DSI does not maintain a permanent all-service operations centre. During ordinary activity, directorates operate through their own duty rooms and the Director-General's cabinet. A temporary national centre is activated during a major terrorist incident, foreign penetration crisis, widespread cyber event or complex executive-protection operation. The centre integrates operational reporting without becoming a standing command above the directorates after the crisis ends.

### Territorial presence

The largest concentrations of personnel are in **Lévane** and **Solane**. Lévane contains headquarters, executive protection, the central analytic and clearance functions, and most counter-espionage liaison. Solane contains the largest counter-terrorism, economic-security and urban operational establishment because of its population, port, corporate concentration and interior-migrant communities. Permanent regional bureaux operate elsewhere, with smaller detachments at strategic ports, nuclear and aerospace sites, defence-industrial concentrations and in the Canary Islands.

Regional directors report to headquarters rather than local prefects. This preserves national consistency but creates a familiar field complaint: Lévane can set priorities without understanding the local consequence of an operation. Headquarters officers answer that independent regional ownership would reproduce the fragmentation the DSI was created to overcome. The most persistent internal organisational conflict is therefore between centralisers and regional officers seeking greater control over sources and operational timing.

### Personnel

The exact establishment is not published. Parliamentary and professional estimates place the DSI between **7,000 and 9,000 personnel**, with approximately **8,200** commonly cited after the 2024 transfers. Around 40–45% possess sworn police or specialised investigative status. The remainder include analysts, linguists, engineers, accountants, protective drivers, communications staff, psychologists, administrative personnel and technical specialists.

| Sworn investigators and operational officers | 40–45% | Arrest, surveillance, source handling, protective and tactical duties |
| Analysts and language specialists | 18–22% | Threat assessment, network analysis, regional and ideological expertise |
| Technical and cyber personnel | 10–13% | Domestic cyber defence, technical security and liaison with DRET systems |
| Executive-protection support | 8–10% | Secure transport, advance work, residences, communications and medical support |

Recruitment draws from police investigators, lawyers, civil servants, linguists, engineers, accountants and selected military or intelligence specialists. A common national-security course covers constitutional law, intelligence handling, evidence, counter-interference, classification and institutional history before specialisation.

The DSI's culture is operational, secretive, legalistic and police-centred. Officers regard law as the mechanism converting state knowledge into legitimate action and take pride that arrests produce identifiable files and judicial responsibility. Operational secrecy nonetheless encourages the belief that outsiders cannot judge decisions without possessing the hidden threat picture.

The old conflict between “cops” and “spooks” did not disappear with SCTAS. It moved inside the integrated service. Intelligence officers prefer long penetration, source preservation and uncertainty; investigators prefer arrest, attribution and closure. The 2024 structure attempts to make that disagreement productive without allowing either profession to deny ownership of the final decision.

## Oversight, law and controversy

### Judicial control

Intrusive DSI methods are authorised by specialised judges, while classified administrative chambers review clearance, investment and preventive-measure disputes. The courts may examine underlying material even where the individual receives only a protected summary. This is genuine review, but not equality of information. Security-cleared counsel may challenge relevance, reliability and legality without necessarily being permitted to disclose the government's case fully to the person affected.

Judicial control cannot remove the judgement inherent in a pre-criminal mandate. A court may reject an unsupported file, but the DSI must still interpret organisation, ideology and capability before review begins.

### Universal citizenship and coercive assimilation

The DSI points to rules forbidding investigation based solely on belief, ethnicity, language, religion or party membership. Its distinctions among ANC currents, MRP politics and armed linkage, and the Réseau Sud political economy are real. Critics respond that the protected universal order still has a cultural location: French is the language of security and senior officers come disproportionately from coastal schools and established institutions. Secular presentation appears neutral, while religious or communal association more often enters the file as information requiring explanation.

The tension is sharpest in the interior and among second-generation migrants in Solane and Fernant. JWH exploits exclusion, foreign networks use community institutions and armed groups hide inside ordinary relationships. Refusing to investigate would be professional failure; treating the environment itself as suspect strengthens the narrative on which recruitment depends.

### Archives and political memory

The 2024 decision to transfer the complete DCRI archive to the DSI remains controversial. The government argued that destruction before review would eliminate intelligence on active networks and make it impossible to identify duplicated or unlawfully maintained files. Civil-liberties organisations argued that a service newly empowered to assess ideological threats should not inherit decades of police political surveillance without independent supervision.

Historical files cannot justify coercive action unless revalidated, and the IGSI samples logged archive queries. No complete account exists of how many records were sealed, retained or destroyed. Information is no longer dispersed, but it now belongs to the institution most capable of acting upon it.

### Technical dependence and the DRET loophole

DRET dependence is officially efficient specialisation. The legal problem arises when domestic restrictions delay DSI collection but foreign collection reveals the same domestic contacts. Courts permit receipt of lawfully acquired foreign intelligence while asking whether the DRET was deliberately used to evade a domestic warrant—an intent difficult to prove inside a classified relationship.

### Administrative prevention

Administrative measures may dismantle a network without trial. Supporters argue that the service cannot be forced to choose between prosecution and passivity; critics call the measures penalties imposed through prediction. Clearance refusal is the most common example: it may end a career, while appeal can correct errors without exposing protected sources.

### Executive intimacy

Presidential appointment of the Director-General and complete executive protection give the DSI a relationship with the Presidency deeper than its ministerial status suggests. There is no established evidence that protective information has been used for political intelligence. The possibility shapes institutional behaviour nonetheless. A President knows that the service responsible for political-security assessment also controls personal safety; the service knows that its director serves at presidential confidence.

Strategic priorities may be presidential, but case selection must remain professional and reviewable. The distinction has survived changes of government, though it rests on convention, recorded procedure and corps culture more than structural insulation.

### Anticipatory force

GISI's use-of-force doctrine attracts less routine attention than surveillance but presents a sharper legal issue. The unit may act on credible intelligence that an armed suspect is preparing immediate violence without waiting for the same visible threat required during an ordinary police encounter. The state argues that additional verified knowledge changes what a reasonable officer may consider imminent. Opponents argue that the secrecy of the intelligence prevents meaningful public testing when the assessment proves wrong.

Serious incidents are reviewed by the IGSI, prosecutors and the parliamentary committee. Public reporting identifies shots, injury or death without disclosing the intelligence basis. Legality may therefore be established inside the classified state without being demonstrated publicly.

## Assessment

The modern DSI is one of the clearer successes of Marzena's administrative state. The 2024 reform ended a division in which the DSI understood national threats, the DCRI owned urban intelligence and SCTAS disputed who could act. Integration produced identifiable authority, an evidentiary profession, a national intervention capability and stronger oversight, with little immediate loss because the DCRI was already redundant.

Its success is not political neutrality in the abstract. The DSI is neutral between governments more convincingly than between rival conceptions of the republic. The principles it protects possess constitutional legitimacy but emerged from a socially narrow founding class and still distribute familiarity and suspicion unevenly.

The service's legalism is therefore both safeguard and source of power. Judicial warrants, specialised police status, evidentiary separation, classified appeals and parliamentary inspection make DSI action more accountable than unstructured political policing. The same machinery enables the state to impose exclusions, restrictions and surveillance before an ordinary offence can be proved. Marzena has not resolved that contradiction; it has professionalised it.

The DSI's institutional claim is that a democratic republic need not wait passively for espionage, terrorism or coercive parallel authority to mature into catastrophe. Its opponents do not seriously dispute the existence of those threats. They dispute who decides when dissent has crossed into danger and how a citizen may answer a judgement whose evidence remains secret. The service's durability depends on maintaining the distinction its own history repeatedly blurred: between protecting the constitutional conditions of political competition and protecting the state from the political consequences of that competition.
