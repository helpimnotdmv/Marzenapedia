# Forces Armées Marzenières

The **Forces Armées Marzenières** (FAM; Marzenian Armed Forces) are the national military of the [[Republic of Marzena]]. Established at independence on 2 May 1952, they comprise the [[Armée de Terre]], [[Marine Nationale Marzenière]] and [[Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace]], together with joint commands and common services. Their responsibilities include territorial defence, protection of national strategic capabilities and maritime communications, regional security operations and authorised assistance to civilian authorities.

The FAM developed alongside Marzena's industrial and administrative institutions. Defence requirements helped establish domestic ammunition production, naval construction, electronics, missile engineering and transport infrastructure. Military autonomy consequently depends on a wider system of public agencies, industrial firms, technical education and civilian mobilisation. The armed forces are a principal institution through which the Republic has pursued technological competence and freedom of political decision.

Marzena maintains a professional force with a substantial, dependable operational core. Its strongest capabilities include integrated air defence, electronic warfare, conventional missiles, submarine operations and the coordination of joint formations. These support both national defence and a selective regional projection role, particularly in the Atlantic and Francophone West Africa. Readiness is less uniform across the full establishment: deployed units and elite formations receive priority, while some supporting and territorial elements operate older equipment or train less frequently.

The same force must prepare for a major continental conflict, defend the Canary Islands, maintain Sahel deployments and support the response to the Interior Insurgency. Contemporary defence debate concerns the distribution of resources between these commitments, the cost of maintaining nationally modified equipment and the replacement of ageing fleets. Political support for strategic autonomy and regional influence nevertheless remains broad.

## Constitutional position and missions

*Main articles: [[National Command Structure of the FAM]] and [[Ministère de la Défense Nationale]].*

The FAM are subordinate to civilian constitutional authority. The President of the Republic, as *Chef des Armées*, determines national military objectives, presides over the National Security Council and retains authority over strategic weapons and exceptional deployments. The Chef d'État-Major des Armées (CEMA) exercises professional operational command. The Minister of National Defence directs defence administration, prepares policy, supervises procurement and the defence estate, and manages resources through the governmental budget process.

The Prime Minister coordinates the wider governmental consequences of defence policy, including finance, industrial support, transport mobilisation and civil protection. Presidential authority over operations does not remove their dependence on ministerial administration and parliamentary appropriations. During cohabitation, sustained commitments require negotiation between the Presidency and government within this division of responsibilities.

The National Assembly legislates military status, votes defence appropriations and scrutinises policy through its Defence Committee and restricted proceedings. It has no operational command authority. Civilian control thus combines presidential strategic direction, ministerial administration and legislative control over the legal and financial basis of military activity.

Territorial defence remains the principal force-sizing requirement. Planning gives particular weight to a major north-eastern contingency, the protection of command facilities and strategic infrastructure, and the survival of forces through the opening period of a conflict. Official doctrine avoids formally designating Algeria as an enemy, although geography and force preparation make the principal conventional contingency apparent.

The Canary Islands constitute a second strategic centre. Their defence requires permanent forces, Atlantic surveillance and the ability to reinforce across sea and air routes. Regional operations, principally through the Mission de Coopération Sécuritaire au Sahel, provide training, intelligence, aviation, logistics and selective combat support to partner states. Assistance inside Marzena remains formally exceptional and is conducted alongside civilian security institutions.

## Historical development

*Main article: [[History of the FAM]].*

### Formation and the 1963 reforms

The founding armed forces drew heavily on Marzenian personnel trained in French colonial service, together with newly commissioned officers and locally raised formations transferred into republican service. French professional education, staff procedures and equipment provided an immediately usable foundation. Under President Julien Marest, the Republic combined this inheritance with a national command structure and restrictions on partisan activity within the military.

Early planning assumed that trained officers, familiarity with the northern terrain and tactical initiative could compensate for limited material resources. The 1963 frontier war with Algeria exposed the limits of that expectation. Marzenian units recovered from initial withdrawals and coordination failures, but deficiencies in warning, ammunition, communications and air support restricted their effectiveness. The campaign demonstrated that local competence could not by itself sustain a larger war.

The experience of CEMA Marc Delane and theatre commander Paul Azran became part of subsequent professional study. Reviews examined particular command decisions alongside the institutional conditions in which they had been made. The Commission de Révision Doctrinale, established in November 1963, gave priority to domestic materiel production, air defence, technical intelligence and logistics. It also formalised the principle that operations should be designed around the costs imposed on both forces.

Implementation established much of the later defence-industrial system. Phosphate revenues supported ammunition plants, artillery production, radar, hardened installations and missile research. The FAM continued importing major equipment while developing the capacity to maintain, modify and integrate it within national systems. Foreign campaigns were studied through technical missions and staff-college work, including the vulnerability of exposed airfields and surface ships demonstrated during the 1960s.

The intelligence settlement went further than the other reforms. The creation of DRET in 1966 transferred the military intelligence apparatus into a presidential organisation intended to unite foreign assessment and military warning. Its remit extended into tactical collection, electronic warfare, combat reconnaissance and specialist operational personnel. This concentration improved national warning but eventually left field commanders dependent on an institution whose principal priorities were strategic.

### The Canary campaign and the War of the Sands

Operation CEINTURE DE FER, conducted from 14 to 24 October 1973, demonstrated the operational value of the post-1963 investment. Naval pressure, airborne insertions and amphibious landings established Marzenian control over the Canary Islands, while negotiated arrangements governed entry into the largest population centres. Three Marzenian personnel were wounded; no fatalities were recorded on either side during the campaign.

The outcome depended on Spanish restraint and diplomatic circumstances as well as military execution. Later FAM studies treated the operation as a successful combination of capabilities whose coordination remained heavily dependent on sequencing, communications discipline and relationships between commanders. The campaign preceded permanent joint theatre headquarters and did not establish a generally reproducible model for an opposed island invasion.

Canary incorporation created lasting requirements for garrisoning, maritime aviation, escort forces, sealift and coastal defence. The autonomy settlement of 1983 retained defence as a national responsibility. Military construction and the use of island territory subsequently became recurring subjects of scrutiny by Canarian institutions.

The [[La Guerre des Sables|War of the Sands]], fought against Algeria from **1978 to 1982**, tested the more substantial force built during the preceding decade. An Algerian advance into the disputed plateau corridor developed into a prolonged, artillery-dominated confrontation. Marzenian forces sustained operations more effectively than in 1963, but favourable tactical exchanges did not compel an early political settlement. Mobile air defences constrained the strike force, while repair, ammunition and transport governed the pace of fighting.

A mediated ceasefire confirmed the French-drawn boundary. The war reinforced the importance of industrial endurance and demonstrated the limits of measuring success principally through exchange ratios. Subsequent doctrine placed greater emphasis on deterrent capabilities that an opponent could recognise before hostilities, permanent joint coordination and intelligence responsive to immediate operational requirements.

### Operational intelligence and joint command

The post-war reviews identified an imbalance in the intelligence system. DRET could assess foreign mobilisation, supply relationships and national intentions, yet operational headquarters lacked sufficient authority over collection needed to locate units, interpret local emissions and support engagements. Information useful to a theatre commander could pass through strategic reporting procedures before returning to the force that required it.

The correction during the 1980s returned battlefield intelligence functions and associated specialist capabilities to military authority. These were organised under the **Commandement du Renseignement Militaire et Opérationnel** (CRMO), rather than reviving the former DRM designation. Tactical signals intelligence and electronic warfare, combat reconnaissance and military special operations acquired a common institutional home responsible to the CEMA. DRET retained strategic intelligence and its national collection responsibilities.

The transfer preserved much of the existing professional apparatus. Personnel carried across technical specialisations, reporting conventions and established liaison relationships. The new command retained access to national assessments while developing reporting and tasking procedures around military operational deadlines. Its establishment restored the FAM's ability to develop and act on a battlefield picture without reconstructing the fragmented pre-1966 intelligence system.

Permanent Commandements Opérationnels Interarmées followed in the early 1990s. They combined lessons from 1973, the War of the Sands and foreign expeditionary experience. The services would generate and sustain forces, while joint headquarters commanded assigned formations within a theatre. The distinction reduced reliance on improvised arrangements between service commanders and became the basis of both territorial and expeditionary operations.

### Professionalisation and regional operations

The 1989 constitutional revision expanded legislative scrutiny of defence appropriations. During the following decade, professional enlisted personnel and a stronger career non-commissioned officer corps replaced short-service conscripts in operational formations. The transition retained technical experience within units, improved collective training and made sustained overseas rotations more practicable. It is generally regarded as one of the FAM's most successful institutional reforms.

Under Adrien Vautrin's presidency, defence-industrial restructuring placed production increasingly in mixed-ownership firms while retaining sovereign control over sensitive design and integration. Naval construction and the development of amphibious command ships supported the expanding regional role. The first BPC entered service in the mid-1990s and the second in the early 2000s.

The establishment of the Sahel security cooperation mission in 2007 provided a sustained operational test of joint headquarters, aviation support and distant logistics. Later procedural reforms standardised theatre support, expanded joint planning and introduced common digital reporting. They improved an existing command system rather than creating joint operations anew.

Since 2022, the Interior Insurgency has placed additional demands on reconnaissance, helicopters, protected mobility, engineering and special operations. The resulting workload overlaps with continental and overseas requirements. It has also made the boundary between exceptional military assistance and continuing internal-security participation more difficult to maintain.

## Military doctrine

*Main article: [[Military Doctrine of Marzena]].*

Three principles remain central to FAM doctrine: *défense par déni*, *économie des forces* and *élasticité logistique*. They developed through successive reviews and are taught as related applications of military planning to Marzenian geography, resources and political objectives.

**Défense par déni**, or defence by denial, seeks to prevent an adversary from achieving a rapid or affordable operational success. Plans emphasise surviving the opening attack, preserving command, dispersing forces and contesting the routes and infrastructure on which an opponent depends. Air defence, electronic warfare, missiles, submarines and prepared positions complement conventional manoeuvre. The FAM prepares to defeat limited incursions and reverse local gains without assuming general superiority in an unrestricted war.

**Économie des forces** concerns the relationship between military effort, exposure and the costs imposed on an opponent. Marzenian usage includes selective concentration, concealment, precision and avoiding engagements in which numerical inferiority would dominate. The War of the Sands added an explicit qualification: efficient tactical exchanges do not necessarily produce strategic decision. Deterrence also requires an opponent to understand the capability and political resolve it would encounter.

**Élasticité logistique** describes the ability to change the method of sustainment with distance, urgency and threat. Railways move heavy forces within the mainland; central depots provide reserve depth; theatre logistics provide responsiveness; air transport moves urgent personnel and cargo; civilian transport and industry sustain a longer effort. Maritime supply remains essential to the Canaries and substantial overseas deployments. The doctrine gives civilian infrastructure a direct role in military endurance.

Strategic autonomy is understood as freedom of decision rather than complete domestic production. The associated principle of sovereignty of sustainment seeks maintenance authority, repair knowledge, stocks and alternative sources sufficient to continue operating through supply interruption. Foreign engines, advanced sensors, semiconductors and precision machinery remain important dependencies. Their presence is accepted, while restrictions that could give a supplier an immediate veto over operations are treated as a procurement concern.

## Organisation and command

*Main article: [[National Command Structure of the FAM]].*

The FAM comprises three armed services. Their chiefs recruit, train, equip and sustain personnel and formations. The CEMA balances military requirements, directs operational planning and assigns forces to joint commands. The État-Major Interarmées (EMIA) supports these functions through personnel, intelligence, operations, logistics, planning, communications, training and resource staffs.

Operational direction passes from the President through the CEMA and joint command structure. The Minister exercises civilian administrative authority without routinely issuing battlefield orders outside that chain. Strategic weapon release and politically exceptional operations remain subject to reserved presidential decisions; commanders conduct authorised operations within approved mandates and rules of engagement.

Five permanent joint operational commands cover the northern contingency, southern territory, the Atlantic–Canary area, the Sahel mission and the national reinforcement pool. These are COI Nord, COI Sud, COI Atlantique–Canaries, COI Sahel and COI Réserve Nationale. Most manoeuvre formations, ships and aircraft remain administratively within their services and pass under a COI when assigned. Permanent headquarters and supporting staffs provide continuity between exercises, rotations and crises.

The national command centre at Lévane forms part of a protected communications and continuity network. The Système intégré de conduite opérationnelle (SICO), known during development as Asterre, combines selected intelligence, force-status and logistical information. Service combat systems retain their own functions, including air-defence control. SICO supports shared awareness and coordination; it does not itself confer authority to release weapons. Headquarters train to continue operating through local networks, radio and manual procedures when connectivity is degraded.

## Armed services

### Armée de Terre

*Main article: [[Armée de Terre]].*

The Army is the largest service, with an establishment of approximately 105,000. Its principal responsibilities are continental defence, territorial reinforcement and the provision of ground formations for joint operations. Its organisation reflects the different demands of the northern corridor and the much larger, less densely supported southern territory.

Northern preparations emphasise mechanised formations, artillery, air defence, prepared positions and movement along interior transport routes. The modern heavy component is concentrated and supported by older equipment retained for secondary roles and mobilisation. Southern operations depend more heavily on wheeled mobility, engineers, reconnaissance, helicopters and dispersed support. These requirements coexist within the same service and compete for aviation, maintenance and trained specialists.

Airborne, marine infantry and rapid-intervention formations provide forces for reinforcement and expeditionary tasks. Army aviation supplies rotary-wing mobility, reconnaissance and attack support. Tactical drones and short-range air defence are distributed within the ground force, while larger unmanned aircraft and the national air-defence system remain under Air and Space Force administration.

The Army has accumulated substantial experience in route clearance, protected movement and support to distant detachments. Its continuing challenge is to maintain these operational skills while preserving formation-level preparation for a more demanding conventional conflict. Modern armour, aviation availability and sufficient training outside the priority formations remain prominent requirements.

### Marine Nationale Marzenière

*Main article: [[Marine Nationale Marzenière]].*

The Navy, with approximately 36,000 personnel, combines maritime denial with selective power projection. It protects the Atlantic–Mediterranean approaches, supports Canary defence and maintains the ability to transport and sustain limited forces overseas. Atlantic and Mediterranean administrative commands operate within a single naval organisation.

Submarines, coastal missiles, escorts and maritime surveillance form the core of the denial mission. The submarine arm provides covert reconnaissance, anti-surface capability and conventional long-range strike. Its operational value depends on maintenance, trained crews and uncertainty about deployments rather than a large publicly visible fleet. Surface combatants support escort, surveillance and maritime presence across geographically separated commitments.

The two *Lévane*-class BPCs provide amphibious transport, aviation facilities and joint command capacity. Normally one is available for operations while the other undergoes maintenance, training or reduced readiness. They support Canary reinforcement, evacuation, disaster relief and deployments in West African coastal environments. Their availability gives the Presidency an additional regional option, although an embarked force also requires escorts, aircraft and supporting logistics.

Shipborne helicopters are administered by the Navy. Substantial fixed-wing maritime patrol and large maritime drones are operated by the Air and Space Force and assigned to naval or joint commanders. Domestic shipbuilding and repair sustain considerable naval autonomy, while specialist equipment, crewing and the cost of maintaining numerous classes remain constraints.

### Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace

*Main article: [[Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace]].*

The Air and Space Force, with approximately 49,000 personnel, operates national air defence, combat aviation, transport, aerial refuelling, fixed-wing maritime patrol and medium- and long-endurance unmanned aircraft. It also provides the administrative home of the missile command and coordinates military requirements with the national space programme.

The integrated air-defence system is among the FAM's strongest capabilities. Fixed and mobile radars, ground-based interceptors, alert aircraft, naval sensors and the small Horizon Aérien airborne-warning fleet contribute to a recognised air picture. Hardened installations, alternate operating sites and redundant communications support survival during an opening attack. Coverage is concentrated around principal population, military and industrial areas; southern deployments rely more heavily on mobile reinforcement.

Air transport, surveillance and communications connect the mainland, Canaries and Sahel. Their importance extends beyond the size of individual fleets. A limited number of tankers, airborne-warning aircraft and heavy transports must support several commands, requiring central allocation and, during larger movements, civilian charter or maritime alternatives.

The service's substantial strike heritage remains visible in its Tornado-MZ and F-4MZ fleets. Their continued operation reflects extensive domestic adaptation and accumulated expertise, but age and support costs make combat-aircraft replacement a major procurement issue. The emerging drone fleet extends persistent reconnaissance and selected strike capacity without resolving that requirement. Military space activity supports communications, imagery and awareness through cooperation with ASM; the civil agency and launch programme remain institutionally separate.

## Intelligence, special operations and strategic forces

*Main articles: [[Commandement du Renseignement Militaire et Opérationnel]] and [[Commandement des Forces Missiles]].*

The **Commandement du Renseignement Militaire et Opérationnel** provides the CEMA and operational commands with military intelligence, combat reconnaissance, tactical signals intelligence, electronic-warfare support and special-operations capabilities. Its central purpose is to connect collection and specialist action to the requirements of an actual or anticipated campaign.

CRMO coordinates the relevant service elements and directly commands its assigned joint formations. Tactical intercept, direction-finding, battlefield jamming and reconnaissance support theatre requirements. Units embedded within service formations remain responsive to their operational commanders, while CRMO supplies specialist standards, assessment and coordination. National strategic collection remains a DRET responsibility, with access and supporting tasks arranged through liaison.

The Commandement des Forces Spéciales (CSOF) forms the special-operations component within this arrangement. It provides special reconnaissance, direct action, hostage recovery and sensitive military assistance through ground, aviation and maritime elements. Its incorporation connects specialist operations to military intelligence and planning. Missions remain within the CEMA's chain; forces assigned to a theatre operate under the authority specified for that deployment. Intelligence assessment alone does not authorise an operation or weapon release.

CRMO maintains close professional contact with DRET. Liaison officers, secondments, compatible reporting conventions and shared technical training preserve access to national expertise. Its assessments retain the emphasis on measured activity and changes in established patterns, but are prepared for operational decisions and shorter collection cycles. Cooperation allows tactical findings to inform national assessment while strategic reporting supplies context to field headquarters.

The **Commandement des Forces Missiles** administers the land-based missile establishment within the Air and Space Force and answers operationally to the CEMA. Conventional missiles provide attacks against command sites, airfields, concentrations and logistical infrastructure, as well as a survivable retaliatory capacity. Air- and sea-launched strategic conventional weapons are coordinated within the wider national command system.

Marzena's nuclear latency is principally historical. It joined the NPT in 1994 and subsequently retired the declared breakout architecture. Missile and space engineering preserve relevant technical experience, but the contemporary force is described in conventional terms and no operational nuclear arsenal is established. Strategic missile release remains reserved to the President, conventionally following National Security Council consultation and transmission through the authenticated military chain.

## Joint enabling services

*Main article: [[Joint Enabling Services of the FAM]].*

Joint enabling services provide functions used across the armed forces. The term is a collective description, not the name of a fourth service or a single superior support headquarters. Each organisation retains its own responsibilities and works with service units and theatre staffs.

The **Commandement Interarmées de la Logistique** (CIL) manages strategic stocks, depot policy and movement priorities. Service organisations retain most workshops and tactical sustainment units. CIL coordinates national resources while COI logistics staffs organise their delivery within a theatre. Rail planning, airlift coordination, sealift arrangements, operational fuel and ammunition accounting connect military requirements to the civilian transport and industrial system.

The **Commandement des Transmissions Interarmées** (CTI) operates common military communications and cryptographic infrastructure, sets interoperability standards and protects networks. It supervises the military infrastructure supporting SICO. AXIOME Systèmes maintains parts of the software through controlled access, while the state retains operational data and authority over communications and cryptographic specifications. Tactical signals-intelligence and jamming functions belong to the operational intelligence system; they are distinct from CTI's responsibility for the FAM's own networks.

The **Commandement médical interarmées** (CMI) provides hospital care, specialist treatment and rehabilitation. Unit medical personnel stabilise casualties; operational headquarters and aviation organisations arrange evacuation; the CMI prepares reception and clinical handover. The national military hospital at Lévane and regional facilities support this system, with deployable surgical capacity available for larger operations.

Other common functions include provost support, detainee documentation, military-traffic coordination and specialist chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear response. Defence estate administration remains within the MDN. Civilian agencies and commercial providers supply much of the geographic, meteorological and environmental information used by the services.

## Personnel, preparation and service culture

*Main articles: [[Military Personnel, National Service and Reserves in Marzena]] and [[École Supérieure de Guerre Interarmées]].*

The active establishment is approximately 228,000, including service formations, headquarters, schools, hospitals, depots and joint organisations. Around 38,000 are assigned to joint, strategic and common bodies. Establishment figures describe institutional size and should not be read as a deployable headcount. Estimates of personnel available for operational assignment are lower, and simultaneous deployment is further constrained by training, maintenance and rotation requirements.

The operational force is professional. Career officers, non-commissioned officers and long-service specialists provide continuity in technically demanding units. Elite and deployed formations receive priority for personnel, equipment and training. This produces a dependable core for planned commitments, although units outside those priorities may require additional preparation or reinforcement before deployment.

National service survives as a limited four-month programme within the statutory framework of military obligation. Its small annual intake, broadly in the range of 12,000–18,000, is filled by volunteers in practice. Educational credit, training and opportunities to enter professional service support recruitment. Routine compulsory call-up is therefore unnecessary. Completion provides elementary preparation and a possible route into further service, rather than immediate readiness for complex combat assignments.

The organised reserve is approximately 35,000–45,000. A wider mobilisation objective of roughly 300,000–340,000 personnel within ninety days includes the active establishment and additional recalled or activated personnel; it is not an extra standing reserve of that size. Mobilisation depends on available equipment, refresher training, accommodation and the administrative capacity to turn records into functioning units.

The École Supérieure de Guerre Interarmées at Lévane is the principal centre for advanced officer education and joint doctrine. Campaign studies, inspection findings and operational experience inform its teaching. Reviews are expected to produce changes in training, procedures or equipment requirements. The FAM's record of reform has made this process an important source of institutional confidence, even where implementing a technical remedy takes years.

The annual REMPART exercise tests different combinations of continental defence, Canary reinforcement, expeditionary deployment and command continuity. It includes degraded communications, selected reserve call-ups and civilian transport arrangements. Regular service exercises and operational rotations provide additional preparation. The emphasis on testing practical dependencies reflects the central place of logistics and technical coordination in military education.

Service culture stresses professional competence, political restraint and duty to the Republic. French is the language of command and professional instruction, and secular rules govern official service life. Personnel swear an oath of service to the Republic, fidelity to its Constitution and fulfilment of lawful military duties. Its institutional object is the constitutional state rather than a named president or commander.

Shared remembrance is incorporated into the 2 May founding observances. Readings of the names of the dead and a period of silence accompany unit ceremonies. Service colours and regimental traditions remain, but official presentation is generally restrained: formal drill, simple memorial inscriptions and republican symbols predominate. These practices coexist with public displays of aviation, naval construction and technical achievement.

## Defence industry, expenditure and procurement

*Main articles: [[Marzenian Military-Industrial Complex]] and [[Defence Planning and Expenditure in Marzena]].*

Defence production is carried out through public technical authorities, mixed-ownership firms and private contractors. The Office pour les Programmes de Capacité et le Matériel (OCPM) retains sovereign design, integration and testing responsibilities for sensitive programmes. Procurement authorities contract production, while service requirements are balanced through joint planning and the MDN budget process.

CNAM, Groupe SOMAR, ESDM and Lyngonian Defence Works contribute to land equipment, ammunition, naval construction, electronics and specialised systems. AXIOME supplies selected digital infrastructure and software. State controls protect critical facilities and technologies from unauthorised transfer or loss of national access. Imported platforms remain important, particularly where domestic manufacture would exceed the country's industrial or financial capacity.

The annual defence appropriation is approximately US$16.2 billion, or 4.8% of GDP. This includes the public MDN allocation and a classified annex, while some pensions, intelligence expenditure and dual-use infrastructure fall under other accounts. Personnel, operations and maintenance absorb most routine spending. Equipment replacement, research and strategic infrastructure compete for the remainder.

The procurement problem commonly called the **“-MZ curse”**, or **“Dash-MZ curse”**, concerns the cumulative cost of sovereign variants. National weapons, communications, electronic protection and maintenance requirements can turn an imported platform into a small fleet requiring its own engineering, certification and support arrangements. An initially useful adaptation may generate further dependencies as the original manufacturer and foreign operators move to newer equipment.

The expression is informal and is applied more broadly than the literal suffix. It does not imply that every Marzenian modification has failed: several have extended useful service life and preserved operational freedom. The criticism concerns the point at which additional national control becomes disproportionately expensive to maintain. Combat-aircraft renewal has made the issue particularly visible, but similar questions arise in naval systems, vehicles and digital integration.

No comprehensive settlement has resolved the trade-off. Standardisation can lower costs while reducing control over upgrades or supply; extensive customisation can preserve authority while consuming technicians and funding needed elsewhere. Procurement debate therefore concerns which functions require sovereign ownership and which dependencies the Republic is prepared to accept.

## Deployments, partnerships and oversight

*Main articles: [[Mission de Coopération Sécuritaire au Sahel]], [[Domestic Military Operations in Marzena]] and [[Civil–Military Relations in Marzena]].*

The largest concentration of conventional preparation remains in the north. Permanent forces in the Canaries support island defence and Atlantic operations, while COI Sud covers southern military requirements and assistance connected with the Interior Insurgency. Joint Support Base Juby is a principal logistics and aviation centre for southern activity and the Sahel rear area.

The Sahel mission normally deploys a few thousand personnel in rotating advisory, intelligence, aviation, special-operations and support roles. Larger formations remain available in Marzena unless specifically committed. These operations provide experience and political influence while depending on partner-state arrangements and a sustainable flow of transport, maintenance and personnel replacements.

France remains the most important defence partner. Cooperation includes professional education, technical access, equipment support and regional coordination. Marzena accepts substantial cooperation while retaining national command and avoiding a permanent foreign base. The relationship supports capabilities that would be harder to sustain independently, alongside continuing efforts to preserve control over maintenance, integration and operational decisions.

Domestic military activity remains formally subordinate to civilian-led security arrangements. The FAM provides surveillance, protected transport, engineering, communications and evacuation, with selected units also conducting authorised combat operations. Recurrent deployments have made these responsibilities more enduring than the language of exceptional assistance suggests. Disputes concern operational authority, accountability and the use of military capabilities in settings governed by civilian constitutional protections.

Military personnel are subject to service discipline and national judicial authority. Ordinary serious crimes remain matters for civilian courts, and personnel are required to refuse manifestly unlawful orders. Operational secrecy, joint security activity and restricted evidence can complicate the investigation of alleged misconduct. Existing controversies remain subject to disagreement over both individual responsibility and institutional procedure.

The FAM has not functioned as an electoral or coup-making institution. Its constitutional loyalty coexists with a professional outlook close to the Republic's unitary, secular and technocratic state tradition. Parliamentary committees, the Inspection Générale des Armées and ministerial supervision provide meaningful scrutiny, although access to classified programmes and operations remains limited. Defence reporting is broadly tolerated, with tighter restrictions around intelligence sources, strategic targeting and active operations.

Procurement and readiness are the most persistent subjects of public debate. The ammunition-transfer controversy after 2022 added questions about executive disclosure, stock certification and the time required to replenish usable reserves. Domestic operations and military infrastructure have generated further scrutiny. These disputes have not displaced the broader commitment to an independent military establishment capable of acting beyond national territory.

## Contemporary position

The FAM sustains a combination of continental defence, maritime denial, strategic conventional forces and limited expeditionary capability. Its professional core, technical institutions and joint command system provide credible capacity across these roles. Readiness outside priority formations, ageing equipment and the availability of specialised aircraft and technicians constrain how many commitments can be supported simultaneously.

Political objectives continue to encompass territorial security, freedom of military decision and regional influence. Defence planning consequently seeks to preserve several demanding capabilities within the same establishment and budget. Fighter replacement, naval renewal, ammunition production, domestic rotations and the support of overseas partners remain concurrent requirements. The Republic continues to treat that range of responsibilities as part of its national role, leaving their practical reconciliation to successive budgets, procurement decisions and force allocations.
