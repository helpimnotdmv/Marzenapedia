# Operation Ceinture de Fer

**Operation Ceinture de Fer** (French: *Opération Ceinture de Fer*, “Operation Iron Belt”) was the eleven-day Marzenian military operation that seized the Spanish-administered Canary Islands between 14 and 24 October 1973. The campaign combined a maritime exclusion zone and naval blockade with airborne seizures of El Hierro, La Gomera and La Palma, followed by amphibious landings on Fuerteventura and Lanzarote. The two largest and most heavily populated islands, Tenerife and Gran Canaria, were isolated but not directly assaulted. They passed under Marzenian control through negotiated military entry after the Spanish government decided not to order a general counteroffensive.

The operation produced one of the clearest military successes in Marzenian history. Its limited objectives were achieved without a fatality on either side: three Marzenian personnel were wounded, several Spanish soldiers and civilians suffered minor injuries, and no confirmed civilian deaths occurred during the eleven-day campaign. Marzena established effective control of the archipelago, retained it despite diplomatic isolation and selective foreign restrictions, and eventually secured Spanish recognition in 1985. The 1983 Statute of Autonomy created the institutions through which the islands have since been governed as an autonomous Marzenian territory.

Ceinture de Fer was also an act of calculated territorial coercion. Marzena possessed no generally accepted historical claim to the islands, had received no request for intervention from a representative Canarian authority and treated a fisheries dispute as the public justification for a strategic decision already under consideration. The government promised protection of the Spanish language, local institutions and private property, and invoked the right of the islands to eventual self-government. These claims won some sympathy among Canarian autonomists and governments hostile to Spanish colonialism, but they did not convert the operation into a consensual transfer of sovereignty. Most islanders reacted with caution rather than either resistance or welcome.

The campaign was conceived around a narrow political judgement: Spain was much stronger than Marzena in the aggregate, but the declining Franco regime would not accept the costs and uncertainty of a contested Atlantic reconquest if confronted quickly with an accomplished fact. Spanish naval assets were dispersed or unavailable, military decision-making was divided, and the United States had privately indicated that it would not support a Spanish counteroffensive. Washington did not approve annexation or promise to protect Marzena; its refusal to commit was interpreted in Lévane as the absence of the one form of support Madrid would probably require for an acceptable reconquest. France had detected that a serious operation was approaching, but was neither given the complete plan nor prepared to endorse it.

Within the Forces Armées Marzenières (FAM), Ceinture de Fer is regarded as the first operational demonstration of the reforms that followed the 1963 Marzenian–Algerian Frontier War. Intelligence, airborne, naval, marine and air elements acted to a common design, but declassified records show an improvised command apparatus dependent on sequencing, radio discipline and personal familiarity. Aggressive jamming disrupted Spain while also confusing neutral shipping and Marzenian communications. The modern consensus therefore distinguishes the decisive result from the claim of seamless joint warfare: Marzena created temporary local superiority and ended the campaign before Spain converted national strength into theatre power.

## Background

### Strategic importance of the archipelago

The Canary Islands lie off the Atlantic coast of north-western Africa and west of the Marzenian mainland. By the late 1960s, Marzenian strategic planning treated the archipelago as the principal gap in the republic’s western defence geography. Aircraft operating from the islands could observe Atlantic shipping routes, extend maritime patrol coverage and approach the Marzenian coast from a direction poorly served by the country’s early-warning infrastructure. The principal ports and protected anchorages could support naval operations astride routes connecting Europe, West Africa and the South Atlantic. In peacetime, the surrounding waters contained valuable fishing grounds; in crisis, the islands could either extend Marzena’s strategic depth or sharply reduce it.

This assessment did not initially produce a policy of annexation. After the 1963 frontier war, the FAM concentrated on Algeria, ammunition reserves, artillery, airfield protection and strategic warning. Atlantic planning remained subordinate to defence of the mainland. The naval staff nevertheless began to study the implications of hostile or politically uncertain control of the Canaries, while the air force examined the difficulty of maintaining surveillance west of the coast without forward bases. These studies were contingency work rather than approved acquisition policy.

The establishment of the DRET in 1966 made it possible to combine those military concerns with reporting on Spanish policy and Canarian conditions. By 1970–71, a classified interservice study had moved beyond the defence of mainland approaches and considered several forms of coercive action: temporary occupation of minor islands, establishment of a maritime security belt, seizure of airfields during a wider Spanish crisis and, at the most ambitious end, control of the complete archipelago. The earliest surviving papers did not describe incorporation as inevitable. They treated the islands as leverage whose occupation might compel a permanent security settlement with Madrid.

The strategic case later accumulated economic claims concerning fisheries, aviation, tourism and port services. Internal estimates warned that infrastructure, garrisoning and integration could cost more than the islands produced for years. Economic expectations supported the operation, but control of Atlantic approaches, airfields and surveillance positions remained decisive.

Marzena had no widely accepted historical title to the archipelago. Government publications referred to geographic proximity, pre-modern maritime contact and the islands’ relationship with the African coast, but even classified legal reviews considered these arguments insufficient to displace Spanish sovereignty. The operation’s later defenders instead combined three propositions: Spain had administered the islands as a distant and centralised possession; the surrounding maritime regime injured legitimate Marzenian interests; and Canarian self-government could be more fully realised within a Marzenian territorial settlement. None supplied a conventional legal right to seize the islands, and the absence of a referendum before the operation remained the principal weakness in the self-determination claim.

### The fisheries dispute

The immediate public crisis concerned fishing rights. Relations between Marzenian crews and Spanish authorities had been strained by licensing, net seizures, arrests and incompatible claims concerning conservation zones. Negotiations periodically reduced tension but never produced a durable regime. During 1973, a sequence of vessel inspections and detentions received increasingly prominent treatment in the Marzenian press. Fishing associations organised demonstrations at Atlantic ports, and the government demanded both compensation and a new access agreement.

Later archival work has shown that the dispute was manipulated. The grievances were real, but they did not explain the scale or timetable of the military preparations. Cabinet memoranda used fisheries primarily as a politically intelligible basis for mobilisation and as a means of placing Spain in the role of initial coercive actor. The final public demands were drafted in terms officials expected Madrid to reject. Naval concentration was described as protection for fishing vessels, reserve call-ups as precautionary measures and exercises west of the mainland as demonstrations of readiness. By the time the final exchange of notes began, the operational plan extended far beyond any concession Spain could reasonably have made at sea.

The government concealed its annexationist objective. Its rhetoric progressed from maritime protection to a temporary security zone; only after the landings did it invoke a provisional political settlement. Friendly governments could therefore accept the grievance without recognising the territorial result, while Lévane maintained that Spain had been offered opportunities to avoid the crisis.

### Spain in 1973

Marzenian planning depended on the condition of the Spanish state. Francisco Franco remained head of state, but the question of succession had become central to decision-making within the regime. Admiral Luis Carrero Blanco, appointed prime minister in June 1973, was expected to protect institutional continuity. The armed forces remained formidable relative to those of Marzena, yet responsibility for land, naval and air action passed through separate service structures, and the political leadership was reluctant to create a crisis that might expose disagreement at the centre of government.

The Canary garrison was substantial on paper. Approximately 15,000–20,000 soldiers, air personnel, naval personnel, Guardia Civil members and other security personnel were distributed across the islands. Most were located on Tenerife and Gran Canaria or assigned to static defence, administration and internal security. The smaller western islands possessed modest detachments. Fuerteventura and Lanzarote held more consequential positions, but none was organised to repel simultaneous airborne, amphibious, electronic and maritime action without rapid reinforcement from the main islands or peninsula.

Spain’s national superiority did not translate into immediate theatre superiority. Important naval assets were under maintenance or elsewhere, air units were dispersed, and transport was not configured for contested reconquest. Concentrating after Marzena occupied airfields and deployed submarines risked losses and wider war. Spanish commanders nevertheless expected the United States to deter an attack, even as American diplomats declined to define such support.

The international context intensified the uncertainty. The Arab–Israeli war that began on 6 October absorbed the attention of Washington, Paris, London and Moscow and placed Mediterranean communications under unusual strain. Marzena did not create this opportunity and had developed its plan earlier, but the crisis reduced the willingness of outside powers to manage another rapidly escalating confrontation. The timing also limited press and diplomatic attention during the first decisive days. Some later Spanish accounts alleged that Lévane had deliberately selected the opening date to exploit the Middle Eastern war. The surviving Marzenian authorisation papers emphasise Spanish deployments and American signals more than the conflict itself, although few historians regard the overlap as irrelevant.

### External signals

The most consequential external communication came from the United States. During exchanges through senior diplomatic and intelligence channels, Marzenian representatives asked whether Washington would support a Spanish use of force in response to a major Atlantic crisis. American officials refused to endorse territorial revision, warned against unilateral military action and made no security guarantee to Marzena. They also declined to promise assistance for a Spanish counteroffensive. The formulation appears to have been deliberately non-committal.

Marzenian leaders treated the absence of a commitment as strategic permission, judging that Washington prioritised Atlantic stability and Spanish succession over reversing an accomplished fact. Spain could act alone, but a contested reconquest would be harder without American intelligence, logistics and diplomatic cover. The language remains disputed: American records emphasise warnings, Marzenian summaries non-support for escalation, and Spanish accounts a concealed veto. No released document shows American approval or a promise to restrain Spain; Washington left Marzena to bear the risk while declining to make restoration a shared objective.

France knew less. French intelligence observed preparations and understood that Lévane intended more than a fisheries patrol, but neither the complete target plan nor the final schedule was provided. Paris formally condemned the use of force after the operation began. In practice, it avoided measures that might make restoration easier, continued essential commercial contacts and quietly deconflicted French naval and aviation activity from the operational area. This combination of disapproval and acceptance reflected the French interest in limiting instability without breaking a relationship that remained important in defence, trade and intelligence.

Britain called for restraint, withdrawal and negotiations while maintaining relations with both states. Although the Gibraltar dispute gave London reason to resist automatic alignment with Madrid, Britain did not support annexation. Its refusal to convert condemnation into direct pressure helped preserve it as a possible intermediary. Algeria denounced the operation as territorial expansion and used it to reinforce its description of Marzena as an aggressive, militarised regional state. African governments divided between hostility to continued Spanish possession, concern about conquest by another African state and reluctance to establish a precedent for changing colonial boundaries by force.

### Canarian political conditions

Canarian dissatisfaction before 1973 concerned economic neglect, central administration, unequal development and the limited expression permitted under the Franco regime. Autonomist, labour and clandestine independence currents existed, but no organisation had demonstrated majority support for separation from Spain, still less incorporation into Marzena. The smaller western islands tended to view Madrid with greater distance and possessed lighter security establishments. Tenerife and Gran Canaria contained the strongest Spanish institutions, the largest urban populations and more developed political networks, making their likely response both more consequential and less predictable.

For several years, the DRET maintained limited contact with Canarian unionists, autonomist intellectuals, commercial intermediaries and exiles. These relationships yielded information on local administration, transportation, police routines and political personalities. A small number of contacts later acted as intermediaries during the first days of occupation. They did not organise an uprising, seize principal objectives or supply the operational access on which the landings depended. Claims that Ceinture de Fer answered a coordinated Canarian request appeared mainly in post-operation propaganda.

Marzenian planners expected passivity rather than welcome. Their political instructions promised protection for Spanish as the everyday language, continuity of municipal institutions, respect for private property and eventual territorial autonomy. Units were ordered to avoid symbolic attacks on Spanish identity, religious sites or local officials. The decision not to assault Tenerife and Gran Canaria reflected military risk, but also recognition that combat in the principal population centres would destroy any plausible claim to be offering self-government rather than imposing conquest.

## Prelude and planning

### From contingency to seizure

Active planning accelerated during the first half of 1973. The DRET’s finished assessments combined diplomatic reporting from Madrid and Washington, military observation of Spanish readiness, information from Canarian contacts and technical material collected with support from the recently created Agence nationale d’interception technique (ANIT). The institutional arrangement was imperfect: ANIT remained separate from the DRET until 1982, and its engineers sometimes disputed tasking priorities with foreign-intelligence officers. Nevertheless, the Presidency received an integrated judgement rather than the divided military and diplomatic reporting that had failed before the 1963 frontier war.

The assessment argued that a temporary window had opened. Spain retained the ability to reinforce the Canaries, but not to do so immediately and without visible preparation. The political centre expected the United States to restrain Marzena and was therefore inclined to interpret military concentration as signalling. The smaller islands could be isolated quickly. If Marzena established a defensive perimeter, seized the eastern and western approaches and offered stand-down terms before Spanish forces suffered deaths, Madrid would face a choice between accepting negotiation and initiating a substantially larger war.

The plan evolved accordingly. Earlier options had envisaged occupation of one or two peripheral islands to force a fisheries agreement. By mid-1973, planners concluded that a partial seizure would allow Spain to concentrate from Tenerife and Gran Canaria and would sacrifice surprise without producing a durable settlement. The revised concept sought control of five outer islands and maritime isolation of the two main islands. Assault plans for Tenerife and Gran Canaria were prepared as contingencies, but political instructions treated them as measures of last resort. The desired end state was negotiated Marzenian entry, not urban battle.

Ceinture de Fer rested on four operational principles. First, the naval force would establish an outer belt around the archipelago and delay Spanish reinforcement. Second, selected airborne groups would take lightly defended western-island airfields and communications sites during one night. Third, marine forces would secure the eastern approaches on Fuerteventura and Lanzarote. Fourth, the government would use the resulting control of five islands to compel stand-down arrangements on the remaining two. Air power would transport and cover the forces, conduct reconnaissance and signal readiness, but not attack Spanish bases unless Spanish aircraft crossed defined engagement lines.

This was a limited plan, not a small one. Between 8,000 and 12,000 Marzenian personnel participated directly, supported by additional naval, air, logistics and reserve elements on the mainland. The operation required near-simultaneous movement across an archipelago more than 400 kilometres from east to west. Ships, transport aircraft and fuel stocks had to be concentrated without announcing the objective. Each phase depended on Spain withholding actions it was physically capable of taking. The economy of force was therefore political as much as military: Marzena applied overwhelming pressure at selected points while leaving Madrid a path to avoid casualties and claim that its restraint protected civilians.

### Command arrangements

The President was the principal political author of the operation. He defined the strategic objective, approved the escalation limits and retained control over communications with foreign governments. The Prime Minister supported the plan but concentrated on reserve mobilisation, continuity of government, market and supply disruption, and the expected diplomatic response. The serving Chief of the General Staff of the Armed Forces (CEMA), whose identity remains to be defined in the broader political chronology, chaired the interservice planning process and translated the President’s political restrictions into military directives.

On 8 October, a restricted Council of Defence formally authorised the operation. The decision did not go to an open parliamentary vote or the full cabinet. Ministers responsible for foreign affairs, defence, the interior, finance and transport received compartmented responsibilities. Some senior officials learned that the islands themselves were the objective only during the final planning period. Formation commanders initially received target packages rather than the complete campaign design.

A temporary **Commandement opérationnel Atlantique** (COA) was created under a senior naval officer trusted by the President and respected by the airborne and marine commands. Its maritime component, **Groupe Naval ATLAS**, combined approximately fifteen to twenty principal vessels and auxiliaries: destroyer- or frigate-sized escorts, patrol craft, submarines, amphibious shipping, converted commercial vessels, replenishment ships and specialist communications platforms. Marzena possessed no aircraft carrier. Land-based aircraft provided transport, reconnaissance, fighter cover and electronic support.

The COA was joint in composition but not a permanent joint headquarters. Officers assembled from service staffs worked through detailed timetables, agreed communications plans and the authority of the operation commander. They lacked a mature common staff procedure, integrated information system or standing chain through which naval, air and land priorities could be reconciled during unexpected events. The campaign’s short duration and tightly sequenced objectives made this survivable. Records show repeated recourse to personal telephone calls, liaison officers and direct conversations between commanders who knew one another from the École supérieure de guerre interarmées.

### Intelligence, reconnaissance and deception

Small reconnaissance and liaison teams were present in the islands for several months before the operation. Their tasks included observing airfields and ports, confirming routes between garrisons and civil facilities, identifying local intermediaries and preparing navigational points. They were deliberately too few to seize objectives themselves. Operational security depended on preventing their presence from becoming evidence of an imminent campaign, and several were placed under commercial or consular cover rather than organised as visible military teams.

Marzenian deception presented the force concentration as an Atlantic-readiness exercise prompted by the fishing dispute. The navy increased patrols gradually rather than sailing as one formation. Civilian and auxiliary vessels moved under commercial explanations. Airborne units rehearsed dispersed objectives without being told that the islands would be attacked simultaneously. Public statements became sharper, but remained consistent with coercive diplomacy. This supported Spain’s existing expectation that the United States would prevent a territorial attack.

Spanish services detected multiple warning indicators: unusual fuel purchasing, reserve transport preparation, naval movement and a rise in Marzenian encrypted traffic. They did not assemble them into a firm warning. Some officers argued that the pattern exceeded normal fisheries coercion; others judged that Lévane was attempting to strengthen its negotiating position. The belief that Washington would restrain Marzena reduced the urgency attached to the evidence. In this respect, Spanish warning failure resembled the Marzenian failure of 1963: relevant information existed, but prevailing political assumptions determined its meaning.

The operation also depended on electronic interference. Marzenian transmitters jammed selected military frequencies and, at critical moments, interrupted links between island commands and Madrid. The jamming was aggressive but less discriminating than later official accounts claimed. It complicated Marzenian monitoring, forced greater reliance on prearranged signals and affected civilian maritime communications. Several merchant vessels entered the declared control area without understanding that a military operation had begun. Their subsequent inspection was presented publicly as enforcement success, although operational logs acknowledged that Marzenian interference had contributed to their confusion.

## Opposing forces and plans

Marzena concentrated local superiority without approaching Spanish national strength. Its forces were organised to secure fixed objectives rapidly, reinforce them by air or sea and avoid a contest of attrition. Spain possessed the larger armed forces, more potential aircraft and ships and the ability to mobilise a reconquest force. Its units in the Canaries, however, were dispersed across seven principal islands and divided among static defence, garrison, aviation, maritime and internal-security functions. The political authority to initiate general combat remained in Madrid.

The 7e Brigade parachutiste did not drop as a complete brigade. Selected battalion groups and pathfinders conducted the initial descents; additional troops arrived by air after runways were declared usable. This reduced the risk of scattering the force and allowed the same transport fleet to deliver follow-on supplies. The 6e Brigade d’infanterie de marine deployed in task groups from a mixture of military auxiliaries and converted commercial shipping. Its lift arrangements were adequate for lightly opposed landings but would have been vulnerable against prepared coastal fire or sustained Spanish air attack.

The Spanish garrisons were professionally adequate and, on the two main islands, potentially capable of imposing serious losses. Their organisation assumed that an external threat would be detected early enough for reinforcement and coordinated defence. It did not anticipate the simultaneous isolation of multiple islands or the political decision to avoid the first fatal exchange. Guardia Civil and police units could secure buildings and control crowds but were not intended to fight marine or parachute formations. Local commanders therefore confronted a mismatch between the honour and legal duty attached to defence and the absence of a clear strategic purpose for isolated resistance.

Spain possessed the mass to attempt reconquest, but not an assured low-risk method after 18 October. Marzena held airfields, watched the approaches with submarines and could reinforce from nearby mainland bases. American intelligence, logistics and political support would probably have been required for an acceptable campaign. Washington’s refusal did not make restoration physically impossible; it made its price and limits unpredictable.

## Course of the operation

### Maritime exclusion, 14–15 October

Ceinture de Fer began before dawn on 14 October when Groupe Naval ATLAS moved from dispersed positions to establish a control line approximately forty nautical miles west of Lanzarote. Rather than declare a legal blockade, the Marzenian government announced a temporary maritime security and fisheries-protection zone. Vessels entering designated sectors were instructed to identify themselves, accept routing orders and submit to inspection if directed. The wording was intended to deny that a state of war existed while achieving the practical effect of a blockade.

At the same time, the Foreign Ministry notified Madrid through a protected diplomatic channel that Marzena was acting to prevent further interference with its vessels and demanded immediate negotiations. The message did not disclose the impending landings. Spanish officials rejected the zone as unlawful and ordered forces in the islands to increased readiness, but the initial response remained diplomatic. The Spanish government still interpreted the naval deployment as coercive leverage.

Commercial traffic mostly complied. Marzenian patrols challenged, redirected or inspected several vessels without firing. Confusion was greater than public reports admitted. Radio jamming affected civilian frequencies, and captains approaching from the south or west sometimes received fragmentary instructions. A small number crossed the declared line unknowingly and were intercepted closer to the islands. The boarding parties behaved correctly and released the vessels, but the incidents demonstrated how easily the attempt to control Spanish communications could produce neutral casualties or an international confrontation.

ATLAS was organised in sectors rather than as a concentrated battle fleet. Escorts guarded the amphibious groups and replenishment vessels, patrol craft enforced the zone, and submarines watched likely approaches. This provided coverage but complicated command. Reports passed through naval channels before reaching the COA, while air reconnaissance used a separate reporting system. Several contacts were duplicated or misclassified. None became operationally serious, but later analysis found that an approaching Spanish force could have exploited the time required to establish a common picture.

During 15 October, Madrid ordered readiness measures at Gando and other military facilities and sought clarification from Washington. The American response urged restraint and repeated that no prior commitment covered a Spanish counteroffensive. The Spanish government did not order aircraft to attack ATLAS or authorise island commanders to open fire except in immediate self-defence. This decision, taken before Marzena’s principal land actions, gave the attackers the political space on which their plan depended.

### Western-island airborne seizures, 15–16 October

The airborne phase began late on 15 October. Pathfinders and small reconnaissance elements marked approaches and confirmed that the selected landing areas remained unobstructed. Transport formations then approached El Hierro, La Gomera and La Palma on separated routes. The objective was near-simultaneous seizure of runways, radio facilities, administrative centres and garrison headquarters before Spanish regional command could understand the scope of the operation.

The landings were not as precise as later commemorative films suggested. One transport element made a serious navigational error and approached the wrong release line, bringing its troops close to a descent over open water. The mistake was corrected late, and the resulting drop was scattered. Several soldiers were injured lightly on landing, though only one injury was initially counted among the three official campaign wounded. The affected group reached its objective behind schedule with the aid of local reconnaissance personnel.

On El Hierro and La Gomera, Marzenian forces surrounded communications sites and small military positions before dawn. Spanish personnel secured weapons and public buildings but received incomplete reports from regional command. Marzenian officers delivered written stand-down terms guaranteeing personal safety, retention of unit records and organised repatriation. No commander was asked to recognise Marzenian sovereignty. The limited formulation allowed Spanish officers to suspend resistance without formally transferring political allegiance.

La Palma presented greater uncertainty because of its larger population and more substantial facilities. Spanish forces established defensive positions around their barracks and temporarily blocked an approach to the principal administrative area. Marzenian commanders avoided forcing the position. Intermediaries contacted municipal officials, while aircraft delivered follow-on troops to the secured runway. By the morning of 16 October, the Spanish detachment had concentrated rather than resisted, and essential civil services were operating under joint local arrangements.

News of the airborne action reached Madrid through partial military messages and foreign reporting. Jamming prevented a coherent early picture and led some Spanish officials to believe that additional drops had occurred on Tenerife. The Captaincy General ordered installations secured and asked for authority to conduct air action against the captured runways. Madrid permitted reconnaissance but withheld offensive engagement. Several Spanish sorties or readiness movements took place; orders to enter the Marzenian control area or strike landed forces were either not issued or later countermanded.

For civilians, the operation was experienced less as battle than as a sudden interruption of ordinary authority. Aircraft crossed at night, radio and telephone service became unreliable, airports closed, and armed troops appeared at municipal offices and road junctions. Temporary curfews were announced in Spanish. Shops and workplaces remained closed through much of the next day. There were no mass demonstrations. The dominant response was to remain indoors, seek information and wait to see whether Spain would return in force.

By the evening of 16 October, Marzena controlled the principal access points on all three western islands. Much of the 7e Brigade had not parachuted; follow-on elements landed after the runways were inspected. This distinction later became important in military study. The achievement lay not in conducting three complete brigade drops, as popular accounts sometimes implied, but in using small airborne assault groups to unlock airfields through which larger forces and supplies could pass.

### Amphibious landings, 17 October

The eastern landings were the most dangerous scheduled phase. Fuerteventura and Lanzarote lay closest to the Marzenian mainland and contained the positions necessary to close the archipelago’s eastern approaches. Their Spanish garrisons were larger than those on the western islands, and Madrid had received more than a day of warning. The 6e Brigade d’infanterie de marine therefore approached behind naval screens and under fighter cover, with instructions to isolate garrisons rather than destroy them.

The principal landing areas were Caleta de Fuste on Fuerteventura and Playa Honda on Lanzarote. Converted auxiliaries and commercial vessels carried part of the force, reflecting the limited amphibious fleet of the period. Landing schedules slipped as craft were reorganised and channels cleared. The friction did not prevent the troops from reaching shore, but records later used at the ESGI concluded that opposition by coastal weapons or aircraft would have produced serious losses among slow, lightly protected shipping.

On Lanzarote, marine units moved rapidly toward the airfield and communications facilities. Spanish troops deployed from their installations, established checkpoints and prepared defensive positions but did not fire on the main columns. A combination of jamming and overloaded channels left several local commanders uncertain whether the instruction to avoid combat applied once Marzenian troops approached military property. Liaison officers carried written terms directly to the garrison. The Spanish commander agreed to restrict his forces to designated areas pending orders from the Captaincy General.

The most serious exchange occurred on Fuerteventura. A Marzenian advance element encountered a Spanish checkpoint whose personnel had received warning of a landing but no usable instructions concerning engagement. Accounts differ on which side fired first. The most accepted reconstruction holds that a Spanish soldier discharged a weapon as a vehicle attempted to reposition, after which several rounds were exchanged before officers intervened. One Marzenian was wounded. No Spanish soldier was struck by direct fire, although two suffered minor injuries while moving into cover.

A second Marzenian servicemember was wounded in a landing accident involving equipment movement near the beach. Together with the airborne injury, these incidents produced the official total of three wounded. The separation between combat and non-combat injuries was blurred in early communiqués, which first attributed all three to “operational action” and later described them as evidence of Spanish fire. No fatality occurred.

The Fuerteventura confrontation was followed by the best-known episode of the campaign: a twenty-two-minute radio exchange between the senior Marzenian landing commander and Spanish Major **Arturo Velázquez Miralles**. Velázquez commanded the Spanish force immediately blocking access to a key military area. He refused an initial demand for surrender, stating that he lacked authority to relinquish Spanish territory. The Marzenian officer reformulated the proposal as a local suspension of movement and weapons use pending orders from Madrid. Both sides retained their positions, Spanish colours remained temporarily in place, and no personnel were treated as prisoners at that stage.

The exchange later became politically useful to both countries. Marzenian histories portrayed it as disciplined psychological command that removed the last obstacle without bloodshed. Spanish accounts presented Velázquez as a professional who preserved the dignity of his soldiers and prevented futile deaths while national leaders decided policy. The surviving recording supports neither a theatrical surrender nor secret collaboration. Both officers were uncertain whether the conversation would be overtaken by air attack or new instructions, and each chose language that allowed the other to stop escalation without publicly conceding the legal question.

By the end of 17 October, Marzena controlled the principal ports, airfields and communications facilities on five islands. Spanish units remained armed but confined at several installations. ATLAS controlled the most important sea approaches, and Marzenian aircraft operated from captured runways. The operation had reached the decisive political point: Spain could still fight, but any counterattack would now begin against established positions and could not be explained as the simple prevention of a landing.

### The decision in Madrid, 17–19 October

The Spanish leadership considered several responses. The services prepared options ranging from air strikes and reinforcement of Tenerife and Gran Canaria to a naval movement against the blockade. Elements of the fleet began to concentrate, and Marzenian intelligence received uncertain reports of a possible approach. In response, the COA armed strike aircraft, repositioned submarines and warned the Presidency that contact at sea could occur before the Spanish political decision was known.

This period, rather than the landings themselves, was the operation’s closest approach to general war. Marzenian submarines lacked a complete picture of neutral and Spanish movements. Air commanders had authority to protect the fleet but not to strike mainland Spanish bases. Spanish air units could damage the exposed Marzenian amphibious force, but doing so risked losses, retaliation and a demand for continued operations until the islands were recovered. Neither side possessed a reliable mechanism for controlling escalation after the first ship or aircraft was destroyed.

Spanish communications within the archipelago were also impaired. Marzenian jamming did not cut every link, but it slowed confirmation, forced relays through civilian or alternate circuits and generated contradictory reports. Madrid could not be certain which installations remained under Spanish control or whether local units had already surrendered. The effect was psychological as well as technical: every delayed message reinforced the impression that the operation was further advanced than it was.

The decisive issue was external support. Madrid sought stronger American assurances and received renewed advice against escalation. Washington did not recognise the Marzenian action and supported a diplomatic demand for withdrawal, but it would not promise intelligence, logistics or political cover for a reconquest. France condemned the seizure while privately urging Spain to avoid a wider conflict. Britain offered to facilitate communications. The simultaneous Middle Eastern crisis made the principal powers particularly unwilling to manage an Atlantic war.

Franco and Carrero Blanco therefore chose not to order an immediate counteroffensive. The decision did not recognise Marzenian authority and was presented within Spain as a temporary measure to preserve civilian life while diplomatic remedies were pursued. Island commanders were instructed to avoid offensive action, secure personnel and records, and negotiate phased departure where Marzenian entry had become unavoidable. By 18–19 October, organised Spanish resistance was no longer a realistic part of the campaign.

### Negotiated entry into Tenerife and Gran Canaria

Tenerife and Gran Canaria were never directly assaulted. Marzenian contingency plans contemplated airborne and amphibious action against selected facilities, but the COA warned that urban combat, airfield defence and the concentration of Spanish personnel could transform the campaign. The President authorised preparations while withholding permission to execute them.

Instead, intermediaries negotiated arrangements through the Captaincy General and Spanish representatives. Marzenian troops would enter designated ports, airfields and administrative sites in stages. Spanish units would retain internal order inside their compounds until transport was available, then depart with personal weapons rendered non-operational and unit records. Municipal government, hospitals, utilities and commercial distribution would continue. Spanish flags were lowered according to local schedules rather than in a single public ceremony.

The first Marzenian parties entered principal facilities under joint supervision. Their small numbers reflected both caution and the need to avoid the appearance of forcing surrender before evacuation terms were settled. Larger follow-on contingents arrived only after Spanish units withdrew from immediate defensive positions. On Gran Canaria, particular attention centred on the Gando military aviation complex; on Tenerife, the transfer of regional command and administrative communications carried equivalent importance. Neither site was taken through combat.

The gradual entry produced an ambiguous legal interval. Spain maintained that its authorities remained sovereign even where they no longer controlled external movement. Marzena described the arrangements as acceptance of provisional administration. Local officials generally continued working and avoided choosing between these claims. This ambiguity reduced the risk of violence and later enabled Spanish officers to argue that they had obeyed national orders rather than surrendered to inferior force.

### Consolidation and Spanish departure, 20–24 October

The final days were devoted to movement, administrative control and the prevention of incidents. Spanish military personnel were transported from the archipelago, most departing promptly aboard Spanish or neutral vessels. A small number—principally individuals with local family ties or technical roles—requested to remain and were handled through separate status agreements. The government in Madrid recovered sensitive records and selected portable equipment; fixed installations and state property passed into Marzenian administration.

Marzena flew additional infantry, engineers, military police, medical personnel and communications teams into the islands. Naval vessels shifted from assault support to patrol and escort. Airfields reopened in stages for controlled civil traffic. Curfews remained in effect around major military sites but were relaxed elsewhere. Radio services resumed under temporary censorship, and the new administration issued directives in Spanish before French-language equivalents were available.

Operational control remained uneven. On some islands municipal officials dealt directly with formation commanders; elsewhere DRET liaison officers, military police and civilian representatives issued overlapping instructions. Supplies accumulated at the wrong ports, transport priorities changed without common notice, and the COA lacked the administrative staff required to govern a population. The absence of resistance kept these defects from becoming a security crisis. They nevertheless contradicted later claims that the same headquarters had moved seamlessly from joint warfare to territorial administration.

On 24 October, Spain completed the organised departure of its principal military contingents and Marzena assumed control of the remaining central administrative and defence sites. The COA reported that its strategic objectives had been achieved. The temporary maritime regime remained in modified form, but the military campaign was declared complete after eleven days. A proclamation placed the archipelago under provisional national administration and promised local consultation and future autonomous institutions. It did not set a date for either.

## Diplomatic crisis

### United Nations proceedings

Spain brought the operation before the United Nations immediately after the declaration of the maritime zone. Its complaint described an armed attack against metropolitan Spanish territory and demanded unconditional Marzenian withdrawal. Marzena answered that it had acted after persistent maritime coercion and that the political status of the islands could not be separated from the wishes and security of their inhabitants. The second argument attracted attention among states sympathetic to anti-colonial claims, but its credibility was weakened by the absence of a Canarian representative mandate.

The Spanish draft before the Security Council initially combined condemnation with a requirement for immediate withdrawal. Negotiations divided members over whether to characterise the islands as ordinary Spanish territory, a colonial question or a separate dispute whose status should not be prejudged. The United States supported criticism of unilateral force but resisted language that implied an enforcement commitment. France condemned Marzena without supporting coercive measures, while Britain sought wording that preserved mediation. The original withdrawal resolution was not adopted in its intended form. A diluted text called for cessation of military action, protection of civilians and direct negotiation but established no mechanism to restore Spanish administration.

Marzena treated the absence of enforcement as confirmation that the territorial fait accompli would survive. Spain treated it as a failure of collective security. Neither interpretation meant that the international community recognised the transfer. Most governments continued to regard Spain as the lawful sovereign or avoided taking a formal position. A small number of friendly states acknowledged that Marzena exercised administration, but very few recognised sovereignty. In diplomatic correspondence, the distinction between *control*, *administration* and *incorporation* remained deliberate for more than a decade.

The General Assembly was more hostile. Several African governments endorsed neither historically colonial Spanish rule nor Marzenian alteration of inherited boundaries by force. Algeria presented the operation as regional expansionism. The proceedings imposed political costs but did not alter control.

### International restrictions

No comprehensive embargo emerged. Marzena instead faced suspended negotiations, restricted defence procurement, delayed credit, higher insurance costs and diplomatic isolation. The pressure influenced policy and investment but remained insufficient to force withdrawal.

France condemned the seizure and limited visible defence cooperation, yet maintained commercial access and rejected pressure severe enough to destabilise Marzena. Washington likewise refused recognition and denied authorising the operation, but would neither turn the dispute into a military contest nor organise a restoration coalition. Madrid saw practical acceptance; Lévane saw confirmation that it had read allied priorities correctly.

### Relations with Spain

Spain did not recognise the provisional administration and refused normal diplomatic relations over the territorial question. Its maps, official documents and parliamentary language continued to identify the islands as Spanish. Madrid maintained claims concerning state property, military installations, pensions and the rights of officials who had departed. Marzena rejected negotiations premised on withdrawal but accepted talks on fishing, trade, family movement and the protection of residents.

The dispute gradually changed character after Franco’s death and Spain’s political transition. Restoration remained a potent national claim, but each year of Marzenian administration increased the cost of reversing it. Islanders developed institutions, careers and commercial arrangements within the new system. Spain’s European orientation created incentives to settle transport, fisheries and legal uncertainty. Marzena, for its part, wanted formal recognition more than symbolic Spanish capitulation and understood that permanent hostility restricted its access to European markets.

Britain eventually became the principal mediator because it had maintained functional relations with both states and had never recognised the legality of the 1973 seizure. This allowed London to facilitate without appearing to reward force. The talks separated recognition from moral approval: Spain could acknowledge the existing sovereign settlement while recording that it had not accepted the means by which it arose.

The 1985 agreement provided Spanish recognition of Marzenian sovereignty, preferential Spanish access to defined fishing grounds, trade concessions and guarantees concerning pensions, property and family movement. Britain witnessed and guaranteed several technical arrangements. The agreement did not declare the 1973 operation lawful, and Spain did not abandon its historical condemnation. In Spanish understanding, recognition was extracted from a situation that could no longer realistically be reversed. Relations normalised formally but remained cold.

## Provisional administration and integration

### Military government, 1973–1975

The end of Ceinture de Fer did not immediately produce ordinary territorial government. A temporary military administration assumed responsibility for external security, communications, ports, aviation and coordination with island authorities. Municipal officials, medical personnel, teachers, engineers and utility staff generally remained in their posts after individual review. Senior representatives of the Spanish state departed or were replaced, but administrative continuity was preferred to wholesale substitution.

This arrangement reflected necessity. The COA had been designed to conduct an operation, not govern more than a million residents across seven islands. Marzena lacked enough Spanish-speaking administrators to replace the existing bureaucracy and had promised not to erase local institutions. Initial directives were therefore prepared in Spanish, often by retained officials, while French became the language of correspondence with Lévane. The practical dominance of Spanish survived even as French acquired formal administrative status.

Private property was generally protected, while Spanish state and military assets entered provisional administration. Disputes over public corporations, concessions and abandoned property lasted into the 1980s. Avoiding general confiscation reassured residents and investors but preserved substantial Spanish economic influence.

Spanish military personnel departed rapidly under the arrangements reached during the operation. Most left aboard Spanish vessels as transport became available. A limited number with Canarian family connections or technical occupations sought permission to remain. Their status was determined individually, and former service personnel were generally barred from sensitive employment during the early transition. Spanish police and Guardia Civil structures were dissolved or withdrawn; local policing was rebuilt from retained municipal personnel and Marzenian cadres.

Residents initially retained Spanish nationality and received a protected territorial status. Marzenian citizenship became available through a phased option rather than automatic imposition. For a period, documentation, consular protection and military obligations remained confused, particularly for families divided between the islands and Spain. The 1985 settlement regularised many of these cases and preserved limited routes for dual family and property rights without establishing unrestricted dual nationality as the norm.

The first weeks brought curfews, port and airport controls, censorship, restricted military zones and selective detention of suspected agents or organisers. Ordinary movement and commerce were relaxed quickly once no insurgency appeared. Normality became political strategy: continued administration was meant to look less disruptive than Spanish restoration.

The restraint was incomplete. Labour actions and pro-Spanish demonstrations were monitored, publications were temporarily suppressed, and organisers could be detained or excluded from strategic areas without an ordinary criminal trial. Several hundred people were questioned during the first year, though only a much smaller group remained subject to long proceedings. Isolated sabotage occurred against communications, vehicles and administrative property. A few politically connected deaths followed episodes of unrest during the early integration period, but there was no sustained armed campaign and no continuous counter-insurgency.

### Civilian rule and delayed autonomy

A civilian territorial administration replaced direct military government after approximately two years, while defence, intelligence and external affairs remained controlled from Lévane. The government established consultative bodies and expanded municipal participation but postponed the promised autonomous legislature. Officials cited security, administrative harmonisation and unresolved negotiations with Spain. Critics identified an additional cause: mainland elites feared that meaningful autonomy would limit access to strategic sites and encourage regional demands elsewhere in Marzena.

Political opinion differed markedly across the archipelago. The smaller western islands, which had experienced the least confrontation and had weaker links to central Spanish institutions, generally adapted more quickly. Tenerife and Gran Canaria produced the most organised demands for elected government, labour protection and linguistic guarantees. These movements were not uniformly pro-Spanish. Many opposed restoration while also rejecting direct rule from Lévane. The distinction between opposition to the seizure and support for a Canarian political identity became increasingly important during the late 1970s.

Marzena invested in ports, air connections, military infrastructure and public administration, but the distribution of benefits was contested. Defence construction created employment and improved transportation while reserving land and facilities from local control. Tourism and Atlantic trade recovered, yet regulation from the mainland favoured firms able to navigate the Marzenian state. The islands’ later special economic arrangements emerged partly from efforts to reconcile integration with the preservation of their external commercial orientation.

The **Statute of Autonomy of 1983** created a territorial legislature and executive with substantial authority over tourism, education, culture, local economic policy and municipal affairs. Defence, foreign affairs, strategic infrastructure and national security remained reserved to the republic. Spanish continued as the predominant language of everyday life, while French retained official and higher-administrative functions. The statute was a genuine devolution of power, not merely a change of terminology. It also regularised a decade of control and supplied retrospective legitimacy that had been absent in 1973.

Later Canarian political narratives have treated the statute in different ways. Autonomists describe it as the moment at which an imposed transfer became a negotiated territorial relationship. Pro-integration parties present it as fulfilment of the original promise. Independentists argue that neither Spanish rule nor Marzenian incorporation had been chosen freely and that autonomy cannot substitute for a sovereign decision. None of these positions has produced a renewed international territorial dispute since Spanish recognition.

## Military assessment

### Reasons for success

Ceinture de Fer succeeded because its military design and political assumptions reinforced one another. The DRET recognised the combination of Spanish political hesitation, temporary force availability and American non-commitment. Deception encouraged Madrid to interpret preparations as bargaining. Marzena then concentrated force against peripheral objectives faster than Spain could construct a theatre response. The absence of early deaths made restraint easier for Spanish leaders, while the seizure of five islands made every subsequent response more costly.

The operation also demonstrated real professional strengths. Airborne groups accepted narrow objectives and avoided becoming fixed in unnecessary engagements. Marine commanders isolated garrisons rather than seeking decisive battle. Naval forces enforced a control zone without firing on neutral shipping. Political authorities imposed engagement limits and maintained a diplomatic exit for Spanish officers. Logistics delivered follow-on troops to captured airfields and sustained dispersed forces through the critical days.

These were not merely products of Spanish hesitation. Had Marzena moved slowly, inflicted avoidable casualties or attacked the principal cities, Madrid’s calculation could have changed. The disciplined limitation of force was therefore a military achievement. It allowed Spain’s leaders to choose non-escalation without facing an immediate spectacle of defeat or massacre.

### Command weaknesses

The official image of integrated command is less secure. The COA was a temporary headquarters whose authority depended on the personal standing of its commander and the willingness of the services to accept an improvised arrangement. Naval and air contact reporting was not fully integrated. Amphibious movement suffered scheduling and loading problems. Jamming interfered with friendly and civilian communications. On the western islands, one navigational error came close to causing a disaster. Several contingency actions were abandoned when headquarters could not establish whether the forces required to support them were available.

None of these failures altered the outcome because Spain did not subject the system to sustained pressure. The operation lasted eleven days, most fighting was avoided and commanders could execute a heavily rehearsed sequence. A campaign involving repeated air attack, damaged ports, contested resupply or simultaneous urban operations would probably have overwhelmed the command architecture. ESGI studies later summarised the lesson by distinguishing *unity of plan* from *unity of command*: Ceinture de Fer achieved the former through professional improvisation, but did not possess the permanent institution required for the latter.

The distinction was initially neglected. Political leaders preferred a narrative in which post-1963 reform had culminated in complete joint competence. The services each claimed an essential part of the victory and had little incentive to surrender authority to a standing joint headquarters. Formal Commandements opérationnels interarmées were not established until the early 1990s, after prolonged coordination problems in the later War of the Sands made personal familiarity inadequate.

### Casualties

The absence of deaths was central to both the operation and its memory. Marzena officially recorded three wounded: one in the brief Fuerteventura checkpoint exchange, one during the amphibious landing and one during the scattered western-island drop. Early communiqués did not consistently distinguish combat injuries from operational accidents. Spain recorded several minor injuries among military personnel and civilians but no fatality. No confirmed civilian death occurred between 14 and 24 October.

The casualty figures do not mean that the operation was incapable of violence. Both sides deployed live weapons, Spanish and Marzenian aircraft were prepared for combat, submarines watched sea approaches, and the contingency assault on the two main islands could have produced substantial casualties. The result reflected deliberate restraint, luck and Madrid’s decision not to test the positions, rather than the absence of danger.

## Aftermath

### Marzena

The campaign generated an immediate rally around the government. Control of the islands was presented as proof that the republic could identify a strategic necessity and act without dependence on a larger ally. Ceinture de Fer joined industrialisation, the founding settlement and the 1963 defence of the frontier as a central episode in Marzenian exceptionalism. The operation’s name entered military exercises, speeches and public commemorations as shorthand for precision, secrecy and national will.

Its material consequences were more demanding. The islands required permanent garrisons, protected sea lines, expanded airlift, naval maintenance and Atlantic surveillance. International restrictions reduced access to foreign equipment just as the state assumed a new defence perimeter. These pressures contributed to the **Years of Steel** between 1973 and 1988, during which high military expenditure, strategic industry and self-reliance shaped public finance. The later development of missile, submarine and electronic-warfare capabilities was not caused by Ceinture de Fer alone, but the archipelago made maritime denial and long-range surveillance permanent requirements.

Lanzarote became a major site for technical collection during the late 1970s. Gando on Gran Canaria developed into the republic’s principal Atlantic naval aviation station. Much later, El Hierro acquired additional strategic importance through the national space programme. Each development produced recurring argument over the boundary between national defence and autonomous territorial authority.

### Spain

In Spain, the operation was remembered as a humiliation produced by political paralysis and unreliable external assumptions. Military criticism focused on dispersed readiness, divided service authority, the failure to interpret warning and the government’s refusal to issue clear orders. Defenders of the decision argued that a reconquest could have caused heavy civilian and military losses without assurance of American support or a stable end state.

The absence of combat deaths complicated public memory. There was no lost battle around which to organise military honour and no mass casualty event that made restoration politically unavoidable. Officers such as Arturo Velázquez Miralles became symbols of disciplined restraint rather than surrender. After democratisation, Spanish scholarship increasingly separated condemnation of the seizure from the question of whether a 1973 counteroffensive would have been prudent.

Recognition in 1985 settled the interstate claim but did not eliminate resentment. Spanish governments thereafter treated Marzenian sovereignty as legally established. Political rhetoric occasionally returned to the manner of acquisition, particularly during fisheries or trade disputes, without reopening the territorial settlement.

### Canarian society

For the islands, the operation marked neither uncomplicated liberation nor a simple change of flag. It displaced Spanish sovereignty through foreign military action, preserved much of local society and administration, and began a decade-long struggle over the meaning of the autonomy Marzena had promised. The lack of large-scale fighting limited physical destruction. Political uncertainty, migration, censorship and the reorientation of careers and commerce nevertheless affected an entire generation.

Contemporary Canarian memory is divided by island, generation and political outlook. Some accounts emphasise the avoidance of bloodshed and the institutions created in 1983. Others treat the operation as an annexation later normalised by time and bargaining. Autonomist narratives frequently place the decisive political achievement not in 1973 but in the creation of elected territorial government. The operation remains commemorated by the FAM more enthusiastically than by the autonomous authorities.

## Historiography and legacy

Early Marzenian histories presented Ceinture de Fer as the culmination of the post-1963 reforms. With operational problems classified, they reduced the campaign to the blockade, night drops and landings, and treated negotiated control of Tenerife and Gran Canaria as automatic.

Partial archival releases from the late 1980s onward changed this interpretation. Staff records revealed communication failures, abandoned contingencies, loading problems and uncertainty concerning Spanish movements. Diplomatic documents demonstrated that the American position was not an agreement to transfer the islands. Canarian sources challenged claims of popular invitation. Spanish records confirmed both serious consideration of military options and the extent to which fear of uncontrolled escalation shaped the decision not to use them.

The modern consensus remains favourable to the operation’s narrow military execution while more critical of its political mythology. Ceinture de Fer was decisive within the objectives Marzena selected. It did not defeat Spain’s armed forces, prove that the islands had chosen incorporation or validate a general ability to conduct sustained expeditionary war. It combined intelligence, deception, rapid concentration, limited force and immediate diplomacy so effectively that Spain’s superior national power was never brought fully into the contest.

For the FAM, the operation remains both model and warning. It is studied as an example of shaping an opponent’s decision rather than destroying its forces, and of using air, land and maritime action to produce one political effect. It is equally studied as evidence that professional improvisation can conceal structural weakness. The traditional legend presented Ceinture de Fer as effortless mastery. The surviving record supports a more consequential achievement: a smaller state accepted an extraordinary risk, controlled it narrowly enough to succeed, and ended the operation before its weaknesses could be tested.
