# Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace

The **Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace** (**AAE**) is the air and military-space service of the [[Forces Armées Marzenières]] (FAM). It generates combat aviation, transport, aerial refuelling, airborne surveillance, air-defence and missile forces for national and joint operations. It also operates designated military space payloads and ground services. Its headquarters is in Lévane, with major flying and technical establishments at Lévane, Fernant, Cap-Nord, Solane, Port-Sahar and Kermane.

The AAE provides the fastest means of connecting Marzena's mainland defence, Canary commitments and overseas operations. Interceptors and ground-based weapons maintain national air defence; reconnaissance and command aircraft support the joint operational picture; transport and tankers move personnel, supplies and combat detachments between theatres. Strike aviation supplies a means of intervention before larger ground or naval forces can assemble. These responsibilities make the service central to joint planning even when the principal operation is conducted by another service.

Its organisation combines a technically ambitious mission system with an ageing aircraft inventory. Tornado-MZ and F-4MZ aircraft remain the principal combat types, while C-130 and Il-76 transports receive successive upgrades. Domestic software, electronic warfare, communications and weapons integration have extended their usefulness. Airframe fatigue, specialist maintenance and dependence on imported components continue to determine how much of that inventory can fly.

As of 2026, the AAE has approximately 44,000 active personnel. Seven principal commands administer its forces, with large flying communities organised into wings and smaller specialist squadrons reporting directly to their functional command. Flying formations and airbases have separate chains of administration. The normal overseas contribution is an aircraft detachment with selected crews and support personnel, rather than a complete wing.

## Missions and operating concept

The service's standing responsibilities are the defence of national airspace, the movement and support of joint forces, intelligence and surveillance, and the preparation of air and missile options for the national command authority. It also provides aviation rescue, aeromedical evacuation, state and military passenger transport, and assistance during emergencies. Its military-space organisation supplies selected communications, observation and space-awareness services without controlling the national civil space programme.

AAE planning distinguishes enduring readiness from discretionary intervention. Air policing, warning, rescue and designated transport duties are maintained continuously. Overseas strike, coercive demonstrations and sensitive collection require specific tasking and appropriate political authority. The ability to launch a small force quickly is intended to give the government more choices; it does not confer independent authority on the service to intervene.

The contemporary concept favours a high threshold for offensive commitment and a limited, closely coordinated package once that threshold is crossed. Surveillance, electronic disruption, rapid movement and a credible threat of precise attack are intended to interrupt an opponent's freedom of action without automatically opening a prolonged bombing campaign. Where possible, an operation achieves its purpose through a temporary loss of coordination, a demonstrated vulnerability or the timely arrival and removal of a joint force.

This approach depends on intelligence, mission preparation and the availability of scarce supporting aircraft. Non-kinetic effects are not assumed to replace weapons in every circumstance. Conventional strike, interception and ground-based air defence remain substantial parts of the establishment. Against a sustained attack, the service must preserve its airfields, maintain sorties and continue fighting after the opportunities for a brief intervention have passed.

Three operating areas shape routine planning. On the mainland, the priority is consistent readiness and the protection of government, population centres, industry and military infrastructure. The Canaries require surveillance, air defence, reinforcement and the ability to operate from a geographically separated position. Overseas missions draw on rapid strike packages, specialised reconnaissance sorties and tactical transport detachments, usually supported from a small number of prepared locations.

## History

### Formation and the limits of inheritance, 1952–1965

The independent air service was established on 2 May 1952 from aviation personnel, installations and administrative practices inherited from French rule. Its early organisation retained French-derived ranks, flying-school methods and the distinction between flying units and the establishments supporting them. Aircraft availability depended heavily on foreign technical documentation, instructors and spare parts. The first service could sustain a modest flying programme more readily than an extended independent campaign.

Early missions concentrated on communications, observation, transport and the assertion of state authority over a large territory. The distance between the northern administrative centres and the southern interior made transport aviation valuable before the air service possessed a mature combat organisation. Airfield construction and the training of mechanics competed with aircraft acquisition for limited resources.

Colonel Henri Valette, associated with the consolidation of the Lévane training establishment during the 1950s, argued for a common technical curriculum and an enduring instructor cadre. His reforms were less visible than aircraft purchases, but reduced the practice of tying a unit's competence to a few foreign-trained individuals. The later air academy traced part of its institutional continuity to these arrangements.

The [[Marzenian–Algerian Frontier War of 1963]] exposed the limitations of the inherited force. Warning, reconnaissance, communications and the distribution of supplies did not form a reliable common system. Emergency air deliveries helped isolated formations, including troops supporting the Position 17 sector, but could not repair the underlying failures of preparation and command. Aircrew accounts emphasised incomplete requests, uncertain reception arrangements and the difficulty of matching limited lift to rapidly changing ground requirements.

The war established two recurring themes in air-service reform: national authorities needed a more coherent intelligence picture, and aircraft had to be supported by an organisation capable of turning that picture into timely action. Subsequent investment therefore included radar, communications, transport and technical education alongside combat aircraft.

### Centralisation and the Canary operation, 1966–1977

The creation of the [[Direction du Renseignement Extérieur et Technique]] (DRET) in 1966 concentrated intelligence and technical collection under a strategic institution. Air reconnaissance specialists, electronic-warfare expertise and sensitive mission practices were drawn into the new arrangement. The air service retained the responsibility for many aircraft, crews and maintenance activities while losing much of the independent machinery for setting military collection priorities.

Initially, centralisation appeared to answer the fragmentation revealed in 1963. It improved access to national assessments and encouraged common technical methods. In practice, the requirements of a squadron preparing a mission or a headquarters following a changing battlefield did not always receive the same attention as longer-term strategic collection. The consequences became clearer during the next major war.

During [[Operation CEINTURE DE FER]] in October 1973, the air service supported the seizure of the Canary Islands through reconnaissance, protection of the movement area and the support of airborne and follow-on forces. Its contribution was important to the timing and security of a limited joint operation, rather than an independent campaign for air supremacy. Transport crews and reconnaissance personnel operated within a plan whose success also depended on naval access, local conditions and the restraint of Spain's response.

The operation gave the AAE's predecessor an enduring Canary responsibility. Maintaining an island air position required regular lift, compatible ground support and the ability to reinforce warning and fighter coverage from the mainland. These tasks survived the transition from the initial military administration to Canary autonomy in 1983.

### The War of Sands and operational reconstruction, 1978–1993

The **War of Sands**, fought from 1978 to 1982, confronted the air service with a denser and more dangerous operating environment. F-4 aircraft conducted reconnaissance, protection and constrained strike missions against an opponent whose air defences limited freedom of action. Aircraft could support particular ground operations and impose costs, but could not remove the artillery war or make sustained ground resistance unnecessary.

Operational demands also exposed the gap between strategic intelligence and information required during a campaign. National collection could identify broad developments while leaving air and ground commanders without sufficiently timely detail. Crews adapted routes, profiles and mission preparation to local experience, but those adaptations were not consistently supported by a military institution able to collect, interpret and distribute the relevant information.

Post-war reforms restored military operational intelligence functions through the **Commandement du Renseignement Militaire et Opérationnel** (CRMO). Reconnaissance, battlefield signals intelligence and operational electronic warfare recovered a military chain of specialist direction. Air personnel brought reporting practices, technical qualifications and professional connections acquired in DRET service into the reconstructed system. Sensitive strategic missions continued under separate arrangements.

The same period strengthened hardened support, dispersed operating arrangements and ground-based air defence. The service increasingly treated sortie generation as an industrial and organisational problem: fuel, repair, weapons preparation, trained controllers and functioning runways mattered as much as the nominal combat fleet. Missile development added a separate means of conventional deterrence and complicated the allocation of responsibilities between the air staff and national command.

### Professionalisation, mobility and the Tornado programme, 1994–2009

Professionalisation during the 1990s increased the emphasis on standing qualifications, deployable detachments and repeatable joint procedures. Marzena's accession to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1994 and the subsequent dismantling of its declared breakout architecture also clarified the conventional basis of operational missile planning. Historical technical expertise survived, but did not constitute an operational nuclear arsenal.

General Lucien Arnaud, a transport officer who held senior planning appointments during the 1990s, promoted the treatment of lift and refuelling as national operational resources. Mobility planning increasingly considered the mainland, Canaries and overseas access as one connected requirement. The acquisition of heavy transports and, subsequently, three KC-10 tankers expanded the distances over which a small combat or support force could be employed.

Tornado acquisition followed a broader assessment of the service's strike requirements, maintenance capacity and access to foreign technology. Experience from the War of Sands favoured an aircraft suited to difficult approach conditions and the delivery of weapons against defended targets. Later expeditionary planning added range, refuelling compatibility, electronic warfare and support for independently prepared missions. No single demonstration or late-1980s sortie determined the selection.

Successive British agreements supplied aircraft, spares, maintenance authority and increasing access to systems integration. Deliveries began in 1999 and continued in batches through the following decade. The programme offered an attainable route to a substantial strike force with Marzenian mission systems, but also committed the service to supporting an increasingly specialised national variant of an ageing foreign design.

The 2003 Gulf of Guinea deployment demonstrated the complementary roles of sea and air mobility. Naval forces supplied sustained presence, accommodation and medical capacity, while air transport supported time-sensitive movement between the mainland and the wider deployment. Such operations strengthened the case for a mobility organisation able to assemble detachments for changing missions without permanently dividing scarce aircraft between regional commands.

### Integrated operations and military space, 2010–present

The designation **Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace** was adopted in 2010, before Marzena possessed an indigenous orbital launch capability. It expressed an intended military responsibility for space services as well as an existing dependence on satellite communications and externally obtained information. The initial space organisation consisted largely of ground personnel, liaison officers and service planners. The first national orbital launch in 2019 gave that designation a more substantial technical basis.

During the 2010s, SICO integration, revised personnel records, common mission planning and national communications interfaces improved the service's participation in joint operations. The changes did not make every aircraft physically identical. They allowed different fleets and ground organisations to exchange a more coherent operational picture and work within common tasking and reporting arrangements.

The period also saw the entry of the C-130-derived **Horizon Aérien** warning aircraft, a more visible military reconnaissance establishment and the early operational deployment of the **Aigle-4** family. These programmes expanded the service's ability to find, interpret and influence events. They also increased demand for mission-system technicians, intelligence specialists and software support, occupations already under pressure from civilian employers.

#### The Kati–Bamako operation, 18–19 August 2020

The most frequently cited demonstration of the contemporary approach took place during the Malian crisis of August 2020. On 18 August, Malian soldiers detained President Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta and Prime Minister Boubou Cissé. The military camp at Kati became a centre of the coup leadership's activity. Marzenian authorities subsequently authorised a limited operation during the night of 18–19 August.

Two Tornado-MZ aircraft, configured for strike and electronic warfare, approached the Kati garrison at very low level. The pair initiated a steep climb before descending directly over the military compound at approximately Mach 1.2. They released no weapons. The pass was intended to demonstrate the vulnerability of the compound and disrupt the garrison's immediate coordination; later Marzenian accounts described confusion among personnel at the site without claiming that the Malian military as a whole had been disabled.

During the same operation, Marzenian C-130 transports and personnel assigned through CSOF conducted a restricted movement at Modibo Keita International Airport under dark-run conditions. The participating special-operations unit, the people or material moved and the exact relationship between the airport activity and the Kati demonstration remain classified. Public accounts establish the coordinated use of combat aviation, transport and special operations, but do not provide a complete mission chronology.

The transport and strike aircraft had cleared Malian airspace before dawn. Marzenian diplomatic channels maintained that the Republic was neutral regarding Mali's internal political settlement, while warning that interference with Marzenian interests would provoke a response. The distinction between that stated neutrality and the use of coercive military force became part of the episode's political controversy.

Within the AAE, the operation was presented as an example of a small force creating a temporary opportunity for a joint task through speed, surprise and coordinated effects. It did not establish a general ability to control events in Mali, and the publicly available account does not support claims that electronic warfare alone paralysed the coup. Its lasting institutional significance lay in the coordination of different air communities around a restricted objective.

Subsequent reform consolidated mobility, separated surveillance from the combat-aircraft command and established a clearer public outline for specialist units. Fixed-wing maritime aviation and its dedicated unmanned component passed out of the AAE establishment during the 2024–2025 reorganisation. The present structure retains joint support to naval operations through air defence, lift, refuelling, surveillance and designated strike missions.

## Command and organisation

### National and service authority

The President establishes strategic direction and retains reserved release authority for weapons and operations assigned to that level. The CEMA conducts military operations through the État-major interarmées and the relevant **Commandements opérationnels interarmées** (COIs). The Minister of National Defence supervises the service's administration, resources and preparation within the government's responsibilities.

The **Chef d'état-major de l'Armée de l'Air et de l'Espace** is responsible for generating and sustaining the service's forces. The air staff sets readiness requirements, manages personnel and equipment, prepares force contributions and represents air and military-space requirements in joint planning. It does not operate a separate overseas command chain alongside the CEMA.

A flying squadron retains its administrative parent when aircraft are assigned to a COI or joint operation. The receiving operational headquarters specifies the mission within its authority; the service continues to provide airworthiness, training, maintenance and professional supervision. National air-defence duties use standing command arrangements and delegated authorities rather than requiring a new deployment order for every alert sortie.

Its permanent test unit works with operational formations, OCPM and the national test infrastructure. Airfield engineering, dedicated repair establishments and the common base-support system are also managed through the air staff's technical and support directorates.

The hierarchy is deliberately mixed. Combat fleets and the larger transport, unmanned and training communities use **escadres**, or wings. Smaller formations such as the tanker, heavy-transport and warning squadrons report directly to their functional command. A wing provides continuity for a substantial aircraft community; it is not a standard package that must accompany every deployment.

### Operational allocation and joint integration

The mainland air-defence system supports national standing duties and COI Nord. Southern operations draw on Port-Sahar, Juby and forces assigned to COI Sud. COI Atlantique–Canaries receives aircraft and support according to island readiness and reinforcement requirements. Overseas detachments normally operate through the relevant COI, including COI Sahel, with national assets allocated through the joint staff.

SICO is well integrated into the service's command and reporting arrangements. Air tasking, logistics status and relevant tracks contribute to the joint operational picture through national interfaces. Specialist air-control and weapon systems retain the processing and control functions required for their own missions. A shared track does not itself authorise engagement, and a joint display does not replace the qualified controller or commander responsible for a decision.

The CDA controls the national air-defence activity for which it has standing authority, drawing on aircraft generated by CFAC and CSRA. The warning squadron belongs administratively to CSRA, while its aircraft can support CDA air-battle management or an assigned joint operation. This separates the preparation of a scarce specialist fleet from the headquarters directing a particular mission.

CRMO supplies specialist direction and operational allocation for military reconnaissance and electronic-warfare tasks. Public acknowledgement of an AAE squadron does not disclose its collection priorities, capabilities or individual missions. Strategic DRET tasking uses a separate special-mission organisation and the appropriate national authorisation; it does not give DRET routine control of the entire reconnaissance or combat fleet.

A base commander is responsible for the functioning and protection of the establishment. A wing or independent squadron commander is responsible for the personnel, aircraft and readiness of the flying formation. Co-location does not make the base headquarters the administrative parent of every aircraft using its runway.

The small **Détachement aérien des Canaries** changes composition with the operational requirement. Fighter readiness, transport or liaison support and occasional specialist aircraft draw on mainland establishments. Additional aircraft can deploy without creating another permanent wing. AAE personnel at Gando work within the joint Canary structure, alongside the Navy's much larger territorial and coastal responsibilities.

Beyond the principal stations, the service maintains prepared dispersal locations and access arrangements with selected civil and military airfields. Their value depends on fuel, ground handling, communications, security and technical support. Dispersal planning therefore includes personnel and equipment movement, not simply an alternative runway on a map.

Base-centred maintenance provides routine servicing, common workshops and local support. Flying units retain aircraft-specific specialists and responsibility for their own technical readiness. Dedicated repair sites undertake deeper work that cannot be reproduced economically at every station. This division allows small detachments to deploy, but limits how long they can remain independent of established support.

## Aircraft

### Inventory and availability

### Tornado-MZ

The Tornado-MZ is the service's principal deep-strike aircraft. Its role combines defended-target attack, low-level operations, electronic-warfare support and the delivery of stand-off weapons. It can support joint action against maritime targets when assigned, without belonging to a maritime patrol organisation.

The national programme developed through overlapping standards. Early work concentrated on communications, identification, navigation and compatibility with Marzenian weapons and planning systems. Later refurbishment addressed structural condition, wiring, support equipment and the extension of useful service. The more recent mission-system standard strengthened electronic warfare, network participation and the preparation and recording of complex missions.

These improvements are cumulative but not identical across every airframe. Software integration with SICO is mature at the service level, while particular aircraft still differ in installed equipment, maintenance status and the missions for which they are cleared. Planning systems record those distinctions so that a nominally available aircraft is not assigned a task requiring equipment it does not carry.

The fleet's strength is the depth of experience concentrated in its crews, planners and technicians. Its weakness is the cost of keeping that experience attached to an ageing aircraft. An upgrade can improve what the crew sees and what weapons it can employ; it cannot erase structural fatigue or recreate a broad international supply base. Deep maintenance and component recovery therefore form a permanent part of the fleet model.

Combat-aircraft replacement remains under study. Marzena seeks a foreign airframe with sufficient access to mission systems, maintenance and national integration. Cost, delivery, access and long-term support have not yet produced an accepted combination. The current establishment consequently plans to sustain the Tornado force rather than treating a replacement as already funded or imminent.

### F-4MZ

The F-4MZ remains an operational interceptor. Its two combat squadrons contribute to air-defence readiness and provide reinforcing detachments, supported by a small conversion unit. Modernised radar, communications, electronic protection and weapon interfaces preserve its usefulness for assigned tasks. Those improvements do not remove the aircraft's age, operating cost or limitations against more demanding threats.

The fleet also supports experimentation. Two aircraft belong permanently to the flight-test establishment, and selected operational aircraft can participate in trials under controlled release arrangements. The service values the type as a platform whose systems and maintenance practices are well understood and for which national modification authority is established.

Experimental work is balanced against operational readiness. A test configuration does not automatically become an approved fleet standard, and an aircraft committed to a lengthy trial is unavailable for routine interception. Stored airframes support parts recovery and selected restoration, but their existence is not treated as an immediately mobilisable additional fighter force.

### Transport and aerial refuelling

Mobility development concentrates on improving the existing C-130 and Il-76 fleets. The programme includes structural work, updated navigation and communications, improved defensive equipment where required, and changes to maintenance and cargo-support arrangements. No additional principal transport-aircraft family has been selected for the present establishment.

The C-130 is the normal platform for tactical detachments, airborne support and movements into less developed operating locations. Aircraft can be configured for cargo, personnel or authorised medical transport. Configuration changes require equipment, qualified personnel and time; a nominally flexible aircraft cannot perform every role simultaneously.

The aeromedical squadron's three-aircraft commitment is maintained at 24 hours' notice. Its readiness encompasses airframes, flying crews, the required medical fit and coordination with clinical personnel. The commitment consumes capacity from the transport wing even when no evacuation takes place. During sustained demand, the mobility command must adjust other tasks or seek additional support rather than count the same aircraft twice.

Il-76 aircraft provide heavier lift and long-distance movement between the mainland, Canaries and overseas support locations. Their value lies partly in carrying loads that would require division between several smaller transports. Heavy-aircraft support requirements also narrow the range of suitable destinations. They complement tactical lift and sealift rather than replacing either.

The three KC-10s form a national pool. Tanker support is assigned according to the operational plan and the availability of aircraft and qualified crews, not permanently attached to an individual fighter or strike squadron. Continued upgrades focus on useful service, communications, supportability and compatibility with cleared receiver aircraft.

The tanker fleet permits selected long-distance operations and provides substantial supplementary transport capacity. Maintaining only three aircraft of a complex type is disproportionately demanding. With one or two normally available, a maintenance event can alter the timing or scope of several planned activities. Aircrew preparation and joint planning therefore include operations for which tanker support is restricted or unavailable.

The passenger and light-transport squadrons complete the mobility structure without duplicating its main cargo fleets. They handle movements poorly matched to a heavy transport, including small official parties, specialists and urgent light consignments. Larger emergencies still require prioritisation, civil charter or cooperation with other transport providers.

### Horizon Aérien

**Horizon Aérien** consists of three C-130 airframes permanently converted for airborne early warning and control. They were acquired for the warning programme and are accounted separately from the transport wing. Their common aircraft ancestry provides some support advantages, but the radar, mission consoles, communications, electrical supply and cooling arrangements make them a distinct operational fleet.

ESDM integrated the mission system around imported radar technology and national communications and data interfaces, with OCPM overseeing the relevant integration and test work. The programme required substantial modification of the host aircraft; it was not a transport cabin fitted temporarily with operators' consoles.

The warning aircraft extend surveillance, support interception and provide airborne battle management. Their information contributes to the recognised air picture and to SICO through the appropriate interfaces. They can reinforce mainland coverage, deploy to the Canaries or support a particular overseas operation.

Normally two aircraft are serviceable. Three airframes do not provide continuous nationwide airborne coverage or a permanently available aircraft in every theatre. Crew duty, transit, maintenance and mission-system preparation constrain the period for which a station can be sustained. Ground sensors and other contributors remain the basis of enduring national surveillance.

### Reconnaissance, unmanned aircraft and special missions

The acknowledged electronic-reconnaissance squadron uses a mixed fleet because different collection tasks place different demands on endurance, operating cost and equipment carriage. Its military aircraft and personnel are public; mission loads, collection priorities and individual tasking are restricted. CRMO coordinates the operational intelligence requirement, while CSRA manages the unit's service preparation and support.

The separate special-mission group preserves a distinct arrangement for nationally directed strategic activity. Its personnel work within restricted compartments, with technical liaison and secondments connecting the group to DRET. Operational reconnaissance units share some professional methods and qualifications but receive military tasking through their own authorised chain.

The **Aigle-4** programme remains an early operational capability. CNAM supplies the airframe, ESDM contributes sensors and data systems, and OCPM coordinates development and evaluation. Mission-specific squadrons concentrate reconnaissance, armed support and electronic collection expertise without assuming that every aircraft can exchange roles without modification.

Southern and overseas deployments continue to inform trials and upgrades. Reliability, sensor availability, communications performance and the supply of qualified operators limit the number of sustainable missions. The armed squadron's existence does not make every airframe available for every weapon or imply that the programme has completed operational development.

Concerns about foreign-origin electronics, including Chinese-origin components in early batches, remain part of the programme's public debate. The government states that critical mission and cryptographic functions remain under Marzenian control. The complete supply chain is not public, and national software authority does not by itself establish the origin or replaceability of every component.

AAE unmanned formations support Army and joint requirements through operational allocation. Smaller tactical systems remain with their service users. The division gives the joint force access to a specialist aviation organisation, but can create friction when a ground headquarters wants immediate persistent coverage and the air component must balance several demands with a small available fleet.

### Rescue aviation and CSOF support

The rescue squadron provides national aviation rescue, specialist recovery and deployable assistance. Its helicopters can support military flying activity, emergencies at remote installations and the recovery of personnel or sensitive equipment. Detachments are also used at classified facilities, where public acknowledgement of the squadron does not disclose the supported activity.

Combat rescue in a contested environment is normally assigned through CSOF and the appropriate joint arrangements. The AAE rescue fleet can contribute within its qualifications and equipment, but does not replace specialist aviation or special-operations teams. Sensitive operations may combine ordinary AAE transport or rescue units with classified personnel without transferring the entire unit into CSOF.

### Training aircraft and testing

The flying syllabus uses an imported basic trainer followed, for the relevant aircrew stream, by an imported advanced jet trainer. Operational conversion then takes place in the designated central unit or approved conversion section. These fleets are maintained for instruction; the present force includes no separate light-attack type derived from the training requirement.

The enlarged CEEA conducts flight testing, operational evaluation and support to new mission-system standards. Its permanent F-4 and Aigle-4 aircraft provide continuity, while other fleets contribute aircraft and specialists for defined trials. The centre works with national industry and test facilities, but does not duplicate OCPM's wider programme authority or the role of manufacturers in design and production.

## Air defence, missile forces and space

### Integrated air defence

The CDA combines ground sensors, control centres, ground-based weapons and assigned airborne assets into a national defensive system. Its long-, medium- and short-range weapon formations protect selected priority areas, including the capital region, northern industrial corridors, principal military establishments and important Canary infrastructure. Coverage is uneven, especially across the southern interior, where mobile sensors and deployed elements carry more of the burden.

Regimental organisation by weapon-system role supports common qualification, technical support and readiness management. Operational control connects individual elements to the defended area and the wider air picture. Local protection remains necessary even when longer-range systems are present, particularly around airfields and essential technical facilities.

The current programme primarily improves the protection of established priority areas. Better networking, equipment renewal, mobility and repair capacity receive greater emphasis than an attempt to cover every part of the national territory equally. The system is intended to survive disruption and continue imposing costs after an opening attack.

Airfield protection is part of this defensive responsibility. Security personnel, engineers, controllers, repair teams and flying units contribute different functions. Ground guards cannot replace air-defence weapons, and a functioning radar network cannot restore a damaged runway. Exercises therefore combine protective measures with the practical restoration of flying operations.

### Conventional missile forces

CFM administers the principal national land-based missile formations and prepares them for employment through the CEMA chain. The public structure distinguishes theatre-range forces, longer-range attack forces and technical support. Its weapons are conventionally armed. Exact inventories, deployment patterns and sensitive facilities are not published.

The force contributes to deterrence by preserving the possibility of attack against the infrastructure and command arrangements needed to sustain an operation against Marzena. In a campaign, its use must be coordinated with aircraft, naval weapons and Army fires. Administrative ownership of a missile formation does not confer authority to select a strategic target or release a reserved weapon.

Strategic release authority rests with the President, with authenticated execution through the national military chain. Convention brings such decisions before the National Security Council. Lower-level conventional employment can be authorised within an approved campaign plan where the relevant authorities have been delegated. Political significance and assigned release conditions remain decisive even when different weapons carry conventional warheads.

The historical association between missile research, propulsion expertise and the former nuclear programme continues to attract foreign attention. The present military establishment does not constitute an operational nuclear force. Technical lineage with civil launch work is likewise distinct from ownership of the ASM's launch vehicles or authority over its programme.

### Military space organisation

The **Commandement de l'Espace** is a small organisation of approximately 600 military personnel. It operates selected military payloads and associated ground services, develops space-awareness products and coordinates military requirements with the [[Agence Spatiale Marzenienne]] (ASM). Its establishment is substantial enough to contain operating units, but remains much smaller than the AAE's aviation or air-defence commands.

Responsibility follows the system and the agreed operating arrangement. ASM retains the civil programme, launch functions and the spacecraft responsibilities assigned to it. CE personnel operate designated military payloads or ground services and distribute authorised products to military users. They do not automatically take control of an entire spacecraft because it carries a military instrument.

The **Réseau Étoile** programme is intended to expand access to national communications and observation services. Its twelve-satellite plan is a programme objective, with rollout under way in 2026, rather than a statement that twelve fully operational satellites already provide continuous coverage. Foreign services, shared infrastructure and non-space communications remain relevant to military operations.

Space awareness helps the joint force understand the availability and vulnerability of services on which it depends. The command's work includes tracking coordination, warning and liaison with operators; it does not imply a large independent orbital combat capability. The early adoption of the service's space designation continues to shape expectations that its actual technical establishment is still growing to meet.

## Doctrine and joint employment

AAE doctrine emphasises a coherent mission package rather than an isolated aircraft sortie. Intelligence preparation, warning, electronic warfare, refuelling, transport, communications and recovery arrangements can determine whether a small combat contribution achieves its purpose. The package is assembled around the task and available support rather than a fixed requirement to deploy every category of aircraft.

For overseas action, the usual unit of contribution is a detachment. Its aircraft come from an existing squadron, with qualified crews and maintainers, while additional support is drawn from the appropriate commands. The parent unit continues training and other duties at reduced capacity. Repeated detachments therefore create a cost at home even when each deployment is small.

Mission orders specify the objective, constraints, coordination requirements and delegated authority. Crews and local commanders exercise professional judgement within those limits. Fast-changing operations do not remove the need to distinguish a target from a protected or unidentified object, to confirm the authority for an effect or to report a material change in the situation.

Electronic warfare is integrated with flying operations and military intelligence. It can obstruct communication, complicate detection or improve the conditions for movement and attack. Its effects depend on the equipment, opponent and environment. A mission account that attributes success to disruption does not establish that all hostile systems were suppressed or that the same approach will work unchanged elsewhere.

Air-ground cooperation uses joint-qualified controllers, common planning standards and liaison with the receiving formation. Army and marine infantry users do not need to own the supporting aircraft to request effects or lift. The air component must, however, translate those requests into tasks consistent with aircraft capability, available support and the operational commander's priorities.

Weapons are described principally by role: air-to-air missiles, precision-guided bombs, stand-off attack weapons, anti-radiation and anti-ship weapons where cleared, and specialised electronic-warfare equipment. National integration provides access to selected functions and mission data. It does not mean every weapon category is available on every aircraft or that all relevant components are manufactured domestically.

SICO supports this doctrine by making information, readiness and tasking more accessible across the joint force. The practical advantage is reduced delay between a recognised requirement and a coordinated response. It rests on trained operators, accurate records and functioning interfaces as well as software. The service rehearses continued operation through specialist control systems and local procedures when wider connectivity is degraded.

### Rank structure

The AAE uses the simplified French-derived hierarchy shared with the Army. The reform was adopted through coordinated personnel measures accompanying SICO's wider introduction. Common records made appointments, qualifications and substantive grade easier to compare; the software implemented the reform rather than creating ranks by itself.

Occupational qualifications, instructor status and technical authorisations are recorded separately from rank. A pilot, controller, engineer or protection officer belongs to the same commissioned hierarchy, though eligibility for a particular command depends on the required professional background.

### Recruitment, education and progression

The **Académie de l'air de Lévane** is the principal officer academy. It provides common military education and prepares candidates for flying, operational, technical and administrative careers. Selection for aircrew training is followed by medical assessment, aptitude testing and the appropriate flying syllabus. Admission to the academy does not guarantee assignment to a cockpit.

Technical recruitment combines military education with civilian engineering, computing and vocational qualifications. The Kermane technical school provides service-specific preparation, while partnerships and recognised civilian courses supply specialist knowledge that the AAE cannot economically teach entirely within its own institutions. Commissioned technical officers and NCO specialists receive additional military and airworthiness training before exercising the relevant responsibilities.

Enlisted recruits complete common military preparation and occupational instruction before substantial supervised work in their receiving organisation. NCO progression primarily selects experienced personnel for further leadership and technical education. Promotion depends on performance, qualifications and the responsibilities to be filled, rather than time served alone.

Aircrew pass from basic instruction into the stream appropriate to their intended employment. Advanced jet preparation supports the combat stream; transport, helicopter and mission-aircraft personnel follow the necessary specialist progression. Central conversion establishes a common type standard before operational units develop further mission experience.

Conversion does not immediately qualify a new crewmember for every task undertaken by the squadron. Expeditionary operations, instructor duties, formation leadership, complex electronic-warfare missions and other specialist activities require further assessment. Mission-system operators, loadmasters, controllers and maintainers have corresponding qualification routes of their own.

Retention of technical personnel is the principal manpower constraint. Experienced avionics technicians, software specialists, mission-system operators and engineers are expensive to replace and attractive to civilian employers. Repeated deployments can concentrate demands on the same small group of qualified personnel. Retention measures therefore combine career progression, technical appointments, education and more predictable use of specialist staff.

Reserve training focuses on maintaining useful qualifications rather than recreating a large conscript air service. Short refresher periods support routine readiness, with additional preparation for personnel assigned to specific reinforcement duties. Returning technicians must regain the required currency before certifying work; prior experience alone does not authorise them to release an aircraft for flight.

## Institutional culture and continuing debates

AAE professional identity is strongly associated with technical qualification, precise preparation and the ability to make a small force useful across a large distance. Flying achievement remains prominent, but the service's dependence on controllers, maintainers, intelligence personnel and ground support gives technical careers an important institutional place. A mission's successful completion is understood to include the recovery of aircraft and personnel and the ability to prepare the next operation.

Its visual and ceremonial language combines French-derived dress traditions with republican insignia, numbered formations and aircraft carrying successive national modifications. Squadron identity is expressed through emblems, records of service and qualified specialisms rather than an extensive recreation of Army regimental titles. The service oath and remembrance ceremonies provide a common framework across flying and ground communities.

Several debates recur. The first concerns **fleet strength and usable strength**. Stored aircraft and assigned holdings give an incomplete picture when airframes, crews or mission equipment are unavailable. Maintaining a large nominal combat fleet can absorb resources needed for tankers, reconnaissance, maintenance and the support that makes a smaller deployed package effective.

The second concerns the **sovereign variants problem**. National software, integration authority and domestic repair capacity have preserved options that a more restricted procurement agreement might have denied. Each additional national configuration can also demand its own engineering evidence, documentation, spares and training. The benefit of sovereignty depends on whether Marzena can sustain the resulting system over its remaining life.

The third concerns **restricted missions and public accountability**. The publication of a special-mission unit's existence allows its staffing and broad place in the military establishment to be understood. It does not answer questions about particular deployments, the division of responsibility between DRET and CRMO or the circumstances in which political authority approved an operation. The Kati–Bamako episode remains a prominent example of a publicly visible effect with a largely classified purpose.

Finally, the service must balance mainland readiness with Canary and overseas commitments. A short operation can still require weeks of preparation, deferred maintenance elsewhere and the concentration of scarce specialists. Its ambition is to offer the joint force timely, discriminating options while preserving the capacity for national defence. Whether that ambition can be sustained depends increasingly on supporting personnel and fleet renewal as well as the sophistication of its mission systems.
