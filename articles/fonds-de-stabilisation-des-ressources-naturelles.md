# Fonds de stabilisation des ressources naturelles

:::infobox
title: Fonds de stabilisation des ressources naturelles
Acronym: FSRN
Established: 6 July 1994
Type: Statutory sovereign fund
Jurisdiction: Republic of Marzena
Headquarters: Lévane
Governing authority: Conseil supérieur du FSRN
Primary revenue: Phosphate receipts
Official net assets (2025): MZF 318.6 billion
Principal custodian: Banque centrale marzenienne
:::

The **Fonds de stabilisation des ressources naturelles** (**FSRN**) is the sovereign resource fund of the [[Republic of Marzena]]. Established in 1994 during the liberalisation of the Marzenian economy, it receives a prescribed share of exceptional phosphate revenue and invests most of its assets abroad. Its primary statutory purpose is to convert resource windfalls into productive and strategic capacity; it must also preserve a liquid reserve from which the state can respond to commodity downturns and severe external shocks.

The fund is generally credited with moderating the fiscal effects of phosphate-price cycles. Its intervention during the COVID-19 crisis sustained critical imports, wage-support programmes and employment in several large industrial enterprises without provoking a balance-of-payments emergency. This record gives it support well beyond the governing [[PNLR|Parti National-Libéral Réformateur]]: the nationalist [[MNP|Mouvement National Populaire]] also regards the FSRN as an essential instrument of sovereignty.

The same features that permit rapid intervention have made the fund one of the most contested institutions of the post-1989 republic. The President and the Council of Ministers may authorise withdrawals within a statutory ceiling without obtaining a separate appropriation from the [[Assemblée Nationale]]. Successive administrations have used this authority not only to offset lost revenue, but also to recapitalise defence firms, guarantee strategic projects and finance security infrastructure. Aggregate accounts are published, but the terms of individual investments, the treatment of repayable advances and a small classified reserve remain outside ordinary parliamentary scrutiny.

As a result, arguments over the FSRN rarely concern whether it has worked. They concern what its demonstrated effectiveness should be taken to justify.

## Establishment

### Liberalisation and the modernisation-accounts affair

The FSRN emerged from the chosen liberalisation of the early 1990s. Partial privatisation, the restructuring of state enterprises and greater exposure to international capital created new sources of revenue at the same time that the strengthened Assembly was attempting to impose more regular control over public finance. The incoming presidential administration regarded annual appropriations as poorly suited to long-term industrial policy. Deputies, including members of the PNLR majority, viewed the proliferation of executive special accounts as an attempt to preserve pre-1989 habits under a formally more parliamentary constitution.

The immediate catalyst was the **modernisation-accounts affair** of 1993. A report by the Assembly’s Finance Commission found that MZF 11.4 billion in phosphate dividends, asset-sale receipts and exceptional enterprise payments had been moved through six special Treasury accounts between 1990 and 1993. The report did not establish that the money had been stolen: most of it was eventually traced to industrial restructuring, import guarantees and redundancy settlements. It did establish that the Assembly could not reconstruct the timing, beneficiaries or legal basis of several transfers from the published budget.

The Presidency responded by proposing a single statutory institution with audited accounts, professional asset management and an explicit rule for phosphate windfalls. The proposal was publicly presented as the abolition of opaque special accounts. Internally, the presidential administration also saw it as a means of retaining strategic spending capacity without returning to the Assembly for each transaction. The resulting compromise regularised executive discretion rather than eliminating it.

The institutional design is most closely associated with **Étienne Varin**, a presidential economic counsellor who served as the fund’s first administrator-general from 1994 to 2001. Varin argued that a resource fund subject to ordinary annual appropriation would become either a second Treasury or a short-term coalition reserve. His critics replied that the proposed solution placed a permanent pool of public money beyond the institution that had constitutional responsibility for voting expenditure.

### Law no 94-17

The Assembly adopted the *Loi no 94-17 relative à la stabilisation et à l'affectation des recettes phosphatières* on 6 July 1994. The law constituted the FSRN as a statutory public body with its own legal personality and balance sheet. It began receiving deposits in January 1995.

The founding law gave the fund three functions, listed in order of statutory priority:

- financing investments designated as contributing to productive capacity, security of supply or strategic autonomy;
- preserving revenue above a reference phosphate price; and
- compensating the state during periods of abnormally low resource income or severe external shock.

The first function distinguished the FSRN from a conventional stabilisation account. It was defended as necessary to convert finite natural wealth into durable national capacity. It also created the legal route through which governments could support projects outside the ordinary capital budget.

The bill passed with PNLR and MNP support. The MSM opposed the withdrawal provisions, while conservative deputies divided between fiscal restraint and support for a strong executive. Several safeguards were added during committee consideration, including an annual withdrawal ceiling, central-bank custody and retrospective inspection. A proposed requirement for prior approval by the Finance Commission was rejected.

## Revenue rule

The FSRN is funded principally from [[SOMAPHOS]] dividends, production royalties and exceptional taxes on phosphate exports. Ordinary corporate taxes and the baseline portion of the state dividend continue to enter the general budget.

Each year, the Ministry of Finance establishes a **reference price** using a seven-year moving average of realised Marzenian export prices, adjusted for inflation, product grade and contracted transport costs. When the realised net price exceeds that reference, 65 per cent of the state’s qualifying excess receipts must be transferred to the FSRN. Twenty per cent remains with the general budget, while the remaining 15 per cent is assigned to mine rehabilitation, transport maintenance and producing-area obligations administered outside the fund.

The formula has reduced the temptation to treat a single high-price year as a permanent increase in revenue. It is not automatic in every respect. The government approves technical adjustments for long-term contracts, stock movements and processing costs, and relatively small changes can materially affect the calculated windfall. The MSM and some independent economists have accused successive governments of lowering deposits in difficult budget years by adopting favourable cost assumptions. FSRN officials argue that a rigid headline-price rule would count revenue the state had not actually received.

Privatisation proceeds were permitted as transitional deposits until 2002 but never became a major part of the fund. The continued use of the broader term *ressources naturelles*, rather than *phosphates*, reflects the founders’ expectation that future mining or offshore concessions might be included. As of 2026, phosphate-derived receipts remain overwhelmingly dominant.

## Withdrawals and strategic mandates

The Council of Ministers may authorise withdrawals by decree for a purpose recognised in Law no 94-17. In any financial year, executive authorisations may not exceed 12 per cent of the previous year’s audited net assets. Exceeding that ceiling requires a supplementary finance law adopted by the Assembly. The decree must identify the statutory purpose and maximum amount, but it need not disclose the ultimate contractor, security classification or full financial terms.

This arrangement allows rapid action but weakens the Assembly’s control over the composition of expenditure. Funds transferred directly to the Treasury appear as exceptional non-tax receipts. Other operations remain on the FSRN’s own balance sheet as loans, preferred shares, guarantees or repayable advances. They may therefore support state policy without appearing as expenditure in the ordinary budget.

Strategic-development operations have included:

- foreign-exchange facilities for essential industrial imports;
- capital and export guarantees for [[SOMAR]], [[CNAM]] and [[ESDM]];
- energy-security investments and supplier deposits;
- emergency liquidity for transport and port operators;
- minority holdings in infrastructure considered resistant to foreign control; and
- limited security and continuity-of-government infrastructure.

The formal distinction between a withdrawal and an investment is politically important. A cash transfer reduces the fund’s reported assets immediately. A loan or equity subscription replaces cash with a financial claim and may leave the headline total almost unchanged. Critics contend that some claims are unlikely to be repaid on commercial terms and should be treated as expenditure. The government answers that strategic returns include supply security, employment and technical capacity, none of which is captured by a narrow market valuation.

## Governance

The FSRN is governed by the **Conseil supérieur du FSRN**. Its membership gives the executive a durable majority. It includes representatives of the Presidency and the Ministries of Finance, Industry, Energy and Defence, together with the governor of the [[Banque centrale marzenienne]], two presidential appointees with financial experience and the administrator-general. The Council of Ministers appoints the administrator-general and may dismiss the officeholder before the end of the nominal six-year term.

The professional staff manages foreign securities, currency exposure and routine liquidity within limits adopted by the council. Its operational reputation is generally strong. However, its independence is limited where an investment has been designated strategic. Council directives can prescribe the beneficiary, instrument, maturity and policy objective, leaving staff principally responsible for execution and accounting. Former officials have described the fund as professionally managed but not professionally governed.

The central bank is custodian of the principal foreign accounts and advises on reserve adequacy, liquidity and currency risk. It may require additional documentation and record a formal reservation against a transaction, but it cannot veto a lawful decree. Relations are therefore characterised by tense interdependence. The bank depends on the FSRN as a second line of external protection, while resisting the inclusion of illiquid strategic assets in claims about resources available during a currency crisis.

### Inspection and legislative access

Meaningful oversight is conducted mainly inside the state. The **Inspection générale de l’État** reviews mandates, procurement compliance and the classification of advances. Its full reports are delivered to the President, the Prime Minister and designated ministers. The Assembly receives a summary after sensitive commercial and national-security material has been removed.

The Finance Commission may question ministers and the administrator-general using the published accounts, but it has no general right to inspect individual mandates. The official-secrets regime also prevents deputies from publicly discussing details received through ad hoc security briefings. The [[Cour des comptes]] examines Treasury transfers involving the fund, but does not routinely audit the commercial merits of assets held on the FSRN balance sheet.

The result is scrutiny without full legislative control. Serious administrative irregularities can be identified within government, yet the public and most deputies cannot determine whether a particular strategic operation was prudent, politically favoured or both.

## Investment portfolio

The FSRN invests predominantly outside Marzena. Foreign placement reduces its exposure to a simultaneous fall in phosphate earnings, the Marzenian franc and domestic asset prices. It also gives the government access to liquid foreign currency during external shocks.

At the end of 2025, the fund reported net assets of **MZF 318.6 billion**, equivalent to approximately 11.8 per cent of gross domestic product. Its published allocation was as follows:

:::table caption: "Official FSRN portfolio allocation, 31 December 2025"
| Asset class | Share of reported assets |
| --- | ---: |
| Foreign sovereign and high-grade fixed-income securities | 49% |
| International listed equities | 17% |
| Foreign deposits and short-term liquidity | 12% |
| Multilateral and external project debt | 6% |
| Domestic strategic debt | 8% |
| Preferred equity and project claims | 5% |
| Classified strategic reserve | 3% |
:::

Approximately 84 per cent of reported assets were therefore external. The domestic share was small by value but politically dominant because it contained the fund’s least liquid and most discretionary holdings.

The published total is disputed. Independent estimates for readily realisable net assets at the end of 2025 ranged from **MZF 257 billion to MZF 276 billion**. The gap arose principally from discounts applied to long-maturity loans to state enterprises, preferred shares without a market price, overdue advances and guarantees counted at their expected rather than maximum cost. The FSRN rejects the lower estimates as liquidation values inappropriate for a long-term sovereign institution.

## COVID-19 intervention

The FSRN’s response to the COVID-19 crisis is widely regarded as its strongest institutional success. Between March 2020 and December 2021, the government authorised **MZF 42.8 billion** in withdrawals and facilities. The programme financed medical and food imports, temporary wage support, port continuity, municipal liquidity and working-capital guarantees for major employers.

The foreign portfolio allowed Marzena to purchase essential goods without exhausting central-bank reserves or relying on an emergency external programme. Rapid guarantees also limited layoffs across state-linked industry and the northern manufacturing corridor. Subsequent inspections identified rushed procurement and inconsistent eligibility decisions, but did not find losses large enough to alter the programme’s overall assessment. Even longstanding critics generally accept that the intervention prevented a health emergency from becoming a currency and employment crisis.

COVID-19 also strengthened the Presidency’s argument for discretion. Most measures were authorised by executive decree and presented to the Assembly after commitments had been made. The legislature later incorporated Treasury-facing elements into supplementary budgets, but it did not vote separately on the fund’s guarantees and asset purchases. What supporters describe as proof that the system can act at speed, critics describe as the moment an exceptional financing procedure became politically untouchable.

## Defence and security financing

Defence industry has made the largest and most controversial claims on the FSRN’s strategic-development mandate. The fund has subscribed to preferred capital, provided import guarantees for precision equipment and supported export-credit arrangements benefiting SOMAR, CNAM and ESDM. These operations are defended as investments in enterprises with commercial revenue and strategic value. Their terms are difficult to compare with market transactions because of the state’s [[action spécifique]], procurement guarantees and the role of the classified [[OCPM]] annex.

Since 2022, the most visible dispute has concerned the expansion of the **Base interarmées de soutien de Juby**, commonly rendered in English as **Joint Support Base Juby**. The installation received a major logistics upgrade to support the *commandements opérationnels interarmées* **COI Sud** and **COI Sahel** during the intensified interior security campaign.

The FSRN approved an **MZF 11.8 billion** multi-year facility for the Juby programme, of which an estimated MZF 8.9 billion had been disbursed by the end of 2025. The government classified the project as continuity and strategic-logistics infrastructure rather than ordinary defence consumption. Part of the facility came from the classified strategic reserve; part was recorded as a repayable claim on the defence budget.

Supporters argue that the base reduced the cost and fragility of supplying COI Sud and COI Sahel, and that logistics capacity has civilian utility in disaster response and regional transport. Opponents argue that repayment is improbable and that classifying the works as an FSRN asset concealed the true fiscal cost of the post-2022 campaign. The dispute also connects the fund to the larger argument over whether security expenditure is displacing development in the interior.

### Classified strategic reserve

Up to 4 per cent of prior-year audited assets may be assigned to a classified strategic reserve, although the reported allocation has usually remained below that ceiling. Eligible operations concern defence continuity, critical technology, nuclear-supply security and emergency access to restricted foreign components. The total size of the reserve is published as a portfolio band, but individual holdings and beneficiaries appear only in a classified annex reviewed by the Inspection générale de l’État.

The reserve is narrow in relation to the total portfolio but significant because it establishes a lawful zone in which neither commercial terms nor policy outcomes can be tested publicly. Its defenders maintain that disclosure would compromise suppliers and national capabilities. Critics argue that the combination of classification and executive appointment makes meaningful accountability impossible.

## Political debate

### PNLR and MNP

The PNLR broadly defends the FSRN, but is divided over its constitutional direction. Presidential and industrial-policy figures regard strategic investment authority as the fund’s defining purpose. Fiscal legalists, concentrated among Assembly deputies and parts of the Ministry of Finance, want tighter valuation rules and parliamentary appropriation for any operation whose expected financial return falls below a published threshold. Both currents defend the reference-price mechanism and oppose dismantling the fund.

The MNP also supports the existing institution, making the FSRN one of the limited areas of durable agreement between the two parties. MNP leaders view foreign accumulation, secrecy and executive speed as components of strategic autonomy. They have generally opposed parliamentary restrictions, while pressing for a larger share of the portfolio to support defence production and domestic industry.

### MSM criticism

The MSM argues that the FSRN socialises commodity risk while directing exceptional gains toward establishment industrial interests. In its account, citizens bear restraint during weak revenue years, but strong years produce foreign asset accumulation and discretionary support for firms already protected by the state. The party also objects to the exercise of fiscal power by unelected managers and presidential appointees without a prior Assembly vote.

MSM proposals have ranged from full appropriation of every domestic mandate to a narrower rule treating non-commercial loans, guarantees and preferred shares as budget expenditure. The party supported the COVID-19 intervention in principle, which has complicated its attack on the mechanism used to deliver it.

### ANC and producing regions

The ANC has made territorial and social equity the centre of its position. It supports a statutory share for municipalities, household infrastructure and public services in underdeveloped regions. Some ANC deputies favour a direct citizen dividend, while others prefer a territorial equalisation fund administered through local government.

Campaigns in phosphate-producing districts go further. Local associations, municipal coalitions and some MRP-aligned civic organisations argue that extraction creates a distinct claim to revenue for communities bearing land loss, environmental damage and restricted water use. This claim conflicts with the unitary republican principle that natural resources belong to the nation as a whole. It also challenges a fiscal geography in which resource wealth is extracted disproportionately from the interior, invested largely abroad and mobilised most visibly to protect coastal employment and strategic industry.

The government notes that the revenue formula already reserves 15 per cent of excess receipts for mine rehabilitation, transport and producing-area obligations. Critics reply that these programmes are centrally administered, inconsistently delivered and too often directed toward export corridors rather than local services.

### Media coverage

[[Le Témoin Marzenien]] has taken a cautious but persistent interest in the FSRN. It accepts the fund’s stabilising function while questioning the valuation of strategic claims and the use of repayable advances. Its reporting has generally sought greater disclosure rather than the abolition of executive investment authority.

## Position in 2026

By 2026, the FSRN remains a substantial national asset and a credible second line of defence against external shocks. Its official balance sheet, professional foreign-asset management and performance during COVID-19 distinguish it from a merely notional reserve. At the same time, the headline asset figure overstates what could be mobilised quickly without losses or political consequences. A growing share of contested value lies in domestic claims whose repayment depends on the same state that owns the fund.

The institution consequently stands at a strategic crossroads. One reform current would separate its functions: a liquid stabilisation portfolio protected by strict withdrawal rules, alongside a strategic-investment vehicle whose spending would require normal appropriation and fuller disclosure. The opposing current would preserve a unified fund and widen its authority to finance energy, defence and technological autonomy. A third proposal, advanced by territorial reformers, would reserve a fixed portion of future deposits for producing regions and interior development.

Each model addresses a real weakness while sacrificing one of the qualities that made the FSRN effective. Stronger legislative control would improve democratic accountability but reduce executive speed. A more activist portfolio could convert phosphate income into durable capacity but make the fund less useful during a sudden external crisis. Territorial allocation could strengthen the legitimacy of resource policy while weakening the national stabilisation pool and intensifying disputes over ownership.

The FSRN’s central political question is therefore no longer whether Marzena should save its resource wealth. It is whether money accumulated in the name of national resilience can remain under exceptional executive control once exceptional action has become a normal instrument of government.
