# Fernant Industries

**Fernant Industries** is the ordnance, sensor, and guided-weapons subsidiary of [[Lyngonian Defence Works]] (LDW) and the larger of the group's two operating companies by revenue and headcount. It manufactures radar and fire-control equipment, naval and land gun systems, combat management systems, and the tactical guided weapons at the centre of the *défense par déni* doctrine. It operates the national gun foundry, and is a single point of supply for gun systems across the Marzenian defence-industrial base.

The published account of the firm's development — reproduced in the *Livre Blanc de la Défense*, in LDW's own corporate literature, and in most trade-press profiles — describes an organic industrial evolution. A marine foundry that cast gun barrels acquired the fire-control radar competence that gun systems require; radar competence supported missile guidance; the foundry that made barrels was adapted to rocket motors and missile airframes. Each step follows from the last, and each was directed by state capability programmes under the design authority of the Office pour les Programmes de Capacité et le Matériel (OCPM).

That account is accurate as a description of the sequence. It is incomplete as a description of the mechanism. From 1966 until approximately 2005, the principal engine of Fernant's capability growth was the systematic disassembly, measurement, metallurgical analysis, and productionisation of foreign equipment — predominantly of Soviet origin, obtained first through battlefield capture and salvage and later through third-party and grey-market purchase, and supplemented by French systems held under production licence whose terms the firm consistently exceeded. The practice was conducted through a dedicated internal organisation, **Atelier 9**, and is referred to in Marzenian official language as *rétro-conception* — a term that appears in white papers and appropriations documents without further elaboration, and which the state has never defined in public.

The firm's engineers describe the resulting house style as *Soviet architecture with French electronics*: rugged, tolerant, over-specified airframes, motors, and mountings, carrying imported or licence-derived seekers, processors, and signal chains. The description is approving in the mouths of the people who use it and pejorative in the mouths of European competitors, who have argued in trade forums and in at least two unresolved commercial disputes that a substantial portion of Fernant's catalogue is derivative of designs it neither licensed nor paid for.

## History

### Industrial lineage: the Lyngana foundry (1898–1963)

Fernant Industries is the direct institutional descendant of the **Ateliers et Fonderies de Lyngana**, the marine foundry and workshop established in 1898 by Théodore Vasseur beneath Cap Lyngon, and of the ordnance division that grew within it. The firm's pre-1963 history is treated in full in the parent article; in summary, the works spent six decades as a marine-engineering house that took military work when it was offered, undertook colonial artillery repair under the direction of the army's Ateliers de Réparation, kept damaged equipment serviceable through the war years, and after independence expanded under state procurement direction from castings and repair into complete naval gun systems and mountings.

What the works possessed by 1963 was a heavy foundry, a precision machine shop, a metallurgical laboratory built for boiler and shaft work, and a workforce accustomed to producing to a military specification handed to it by somebody else. What it did not possess was any design capability worth the name. It made what it was told to make, to drawings it did not originate, and it had never in its history developed a weapon.

### Atelier 9 and the origins of *rétro-conception* (1963–1973)

The institutional origin of Fernant Industries lies in the aftermath of [[La Guerre des Frontières|the 1963 border war]].

The war exposed a material asymmetry that Marzenian planners had not expected to face so soon. Algeria's rapid acquisition of Soviet equipment gave it artillery weight and protected mobility the FAM could not match, and the war's institutional reckoning — the presidential intelligence commission whose classified work concluded in 1965 — identified as a specific failure the republic's inability to characterise the equipment it had just fought against. Marzena had captured, salvaged, and recovered Soviet-pattern matériel during the fighting and in the disengagement that followed, and had no organisation capable of doing anything with it beyond returning serviceable items to store.

The first material of consequence was naval. In the months following the ceasefire the Marine Nationale Marzenienne recovered ordnance from Soviet-supplied Algerian coastal craft — gun mountings, autocannon, ammunition, and the sighting and laying equipment associated with them — some taken from vessels driven aground during the corridor fighting, some salvaged from the seabed, and some transferred from French holdings. The material was landed at Fernant because Fernant had the only crane, the only foundry, and the only metallurgical laboratory on the northern coast capable of receiving it. What was intended as a characterisation exercise — establish what the equipment is, what it is made of, and how it is beaten — became within eighteen months a reproduction exercise, when it emerged that the works could cast an equivalent mounting more cheaply than the Marine Nationale could import one.

The reproduction was directed rather than opportunistic. **Instruction ministérielle 66-114**, issued in April 1966 by the predecessor organisation from which OCPM was later constituted, formally established within the Lyngana works a technical organisation for the examination and industrial reproduction of foreign equipment, funded from the defence appropriation and staffed jointly by the firm and by seconded state engineers. The organisation was designated **Atelier 9** — the ninth numbered shop of the Fernant works, in a numbering series that ran to fourteen. The designation was chosen for its blandness. Atelier 9 appears in the works' internal documentation as a shop like any other, and its function was not listed on the site plan until 1999.

Fernant Industries dates its founding to this instruction rather than to the 1898 establishment of the foundry or the 1998 corporate reconstitution, on the reasoning that 1966 is the point at which the works stopped exclusively making other people's designs and began — by a route it would not have chosen — to acquire designs of its own.

The founding institutional principle of Atelier 9 was not substitution and not autonomy. It was sustainment. The formulation that survives in the shop's earliest working papers, and which is still quoted inside the firm, is *we can rebuild anything the enemy fields*. The reasoning was that in a conflict with an adversary equipped by a superpower, the decisive constraint would not be whether Marzena could design a better weapon but whether it could keep captured, damaged, and orphaned equipment in service without a supply relationship. Manufacture was, in the beginning, a by-product of repair: to remake a broken component you must first understand it completely, and once you understand it completely you can make more than one.

Through the late 1960s the shop's output was unglamorous — mounting castings, breech components, laying gear, ammunition, and the fire-control equipment that the Marine Nationale's Soviet-pattern and mixed-origin holdings required. Its first complete reproduced system, a 57 mm naval mounting with an associated director, entered service in 1971. It was not called a copy. It was called a *modèle marzenien*.

### The Years of Steel (1973–1988)

The Canaries Campaign and the international isolation that followed transformed Atelier 9 from a repair-adjacent technical shop into the centre of gravity of the entire firm.

Isolation foreclosed the alternative of foreign acquisition across most of the FAM's requirement, and the *défense par déni* doctrine that emerged from the same period specified precisely the categories of weapon — coastal and anti-ship missiles, short-range air defence, anti-tank guided weapons, rocket artillery — that Marzena could not buy and did not know how to build. OCPM's response was to designate the Fernant works as the industrial prime for the tactical guided-weapons tier, on the reasoning that the works had the foundry, the clearances, and, uniquely in the republic, an organisation with a decade of experience in deriving manufacturable drawings from finished foreign hardware.

Two Soviet weapon families dominate the period, and between them account for the greater part of the firm's modern guided-weapons and rocket catalogue.

The first is **air defence**. Marzena's exposure to Soviet surface-to-air systems was direct and sustained: the artillery-dominated stalemate of *La Guerre des Sables* was constrained on the Marzenian side by Soviet-supplied Algerian air defence, and the FAM's operational analysis of that constraint was the most detailed body of work the republic possessed on any class of foreign equipment. Atelier 9's work on captured, salvaged, and purchased Soviet SAM components — seekers, cooling systems, gas generators, and launch tubes — ran continuously from the late 1970s and produced the firm's first indigenous man-portable and short-range air-defence programmes.

The second is **rocket artillery**. The Soviet 122 mm multiple-launch rocket system was, of all the equipment Marzena encountered, the one whose logic most closely matched its industrial capability: a simple tube, a simple motor, a simple warhead, produced in volume, delivering area effect at a cost per round the Marzenian foundry could absorb. The reproduction programme that began in 1979 was the least technically demanding and the most operationally significant of the period.

Material supply shifted during these years. Battlefield capture, which had furnished the 1960s programmes, was exhausted as a primary source by the end of the *Sables* fighting and the closure of the land frontier. From approximately 1982 the firm's inputs came predominantly from purchase: Warsaw Pact surplus and end-of-life stocks acquired through African, Middle Eastern, and Mediterranean intermediaries, routed through commercial import channels that did not identify the end user, and paid for from an appropriation line that has never been itemised. The transition from capture to purchase is the most consequential and least documented change in the firm's history. It converted an activity conducted on the debris of a war Marzena had fought into a standing procurement operation conducted in peacetime against no particular adversary.

French systems entered the catalogue by a different route. France remained Marzena's supplier of last resort throughout the isolation period, and a number of French systems were held at Fernant under production licence — arrangements under which the firm manufactured to French drawings, paid a royalty, and was bound by terms restricting modification, re-export, and derivative development. Fernant exceeded those terms consistently. The pattern in each case was similar: licensed production established the manufacturing baseline, Atelier 9 characterised the components the licence did not cover, and a *modèle marzenien* appeared some years later that was not the licensed article and was not covered by the royalty. The seeker technology underlying the firm's man-portable air-defence line, and the airframe and sea-skimming guidance architecture underlying its anti-ship missiles, both entered the firm this way.

France was aware of this. The evidence available in the declassified record indicates that the copying of French systems at Fernant was known in Paris from at least the mid-1970s and was tolerated as the price of Marzenian strategic alignment during a period in which the alternative alignment was on offer. No French government made a formal representation on the matter. The commercial disputes that later arose were brought by firms, not by the state, and were settled without litigation.

### Operation HÉRITAGE and the Soviet inheritance (1989–1996)

The collapse of the Soviet defence-industrial complex produced the single largest transfer of capability in the firm's history.

**Operation HÉRITAGE** — the early-1990s Marzenian acquisition of Soviet submarine and missile expertise — was conceived, directed, and controlled by the state, and its most valuable products went to OCPM. The submarine platform material and the long-range missile material, which constitute the strategic core of the republic's deterrent posture, were taken into OCPM programmes to which Fernant Industries has never had design access. What was released to the firm was the tactical tier: short-range air defence, anti-tank, anti-ship, guided rocket, and the propulsion and energetics competence underlying all of them. This division was deliberate and is the origin of the ceiling on the firm's autonomy that persists today.

Within that tier, HÉRITAGE reached Fernant in three forms.

**People.** Between 1991 and 1995 the firm recruited Soviet and post-Soviet design-bureau engineers on individual contracts, principally in solid propulsion, seeker optics, and airframe aerodynamics. The recruitment was conducted through commercial intermediaries and structured as ordinary technical employment; the engineers were housed in Fernant and at the Tazrant site, held Marzenian residence, and were paid in hard currency. Estimates of the cohort's size range from forty to just over a hundred. It is by common assessment inside the firm the most valuable thing HÉRITAGE delivered, on the grounds that a drawing tells you what was built and an engineer tells you what was tried and abandoned.

**Paper.** Design-bureau documentation, materials specifications, and — most significantly — test and failure data were acquired in bulk. The failure data mattered more than the designs. Atelier 9's characteristic weakness had always been that reverse engineering recovers the final answer without the reasoning: the shop could measure a component to a micron and could not establish why it had been made that way rather than some other way. Twenty years of Soviet test series closed that gap for the systems they covered.

**Hardware.** Complete systems, subsystems, and unfielded prototypes were obtained in quantity. The bulk of this material came through the grey and black markets of the dissolving Soviet defence economy — acquired from institutional sellers with unclear title, moved through intermediaries in the Gulf, the Balkans, and West Africa, and landed at Fernant or Port-Sahar under commercial manifests. A smaller portion was purchased through legitimate state-to-state channels from successor governments. The proportions are not documented and were probably not documented at the time.

The HÉRITAGE cohort's arrival transformed the firm's engineering culture as much as its catalogue. The Soviet engineers were the first people at Fernant who had originated designs, and they trained a generation of Marzenian engineers in the practice of doing so. That generation now runs the firm's missile directorate. The original cohort is elderly; those who remain hold advisory and consultative positions, and the firm treats the succession as long since accomplished. Some of the second generation are the children of the first.

### Corporate reconstitution and the end of the copying era (1994–2005)

The reconstitution of the Lyngana works as the holding company Lyngonian Defence Works between 1994 and 1998 established the group's ordnance-and-systems division as a wholly owned subsidiary under the name **Fernant Industries**. Atelier 9, which had operated for thirty-two years as a numbered shop with no place in the organisational chart, was absorbed into the new subsidiary's engineering directorate and appeared in the corporate structure for the first time.

The extension of the *action spécifique* to LDW by decree in 1999 was directed principally at Fernant Industries. The parliamentary and MDN concern that produced it was not about naval construction; it was about a privately held firm holding classified facility clearances, the national gun foundry, a body of foreign-derived design material of uncertain provenance, and a payroll that included several dozen former Soviet weapons engineers, under no formal instrument of state control beyond a procurement relationship and a minority equity stake acquired in the 1950s.

The period from approximately 1998 to 2005 is treated within the firm as the transition out of *rétro-conception* as a primary method. Three things converged. The HÉRITAGE material was exhausted — a finite body of design and test data yields diminishing returns once the systems it describes have been productionised. The engineering generation trained by the HÉRITAGE cohort was capable of originating tactical-tier designs without a foreign article to measure. And the economics inverted: by the late 1990s the components that determined a modern weapon's performance were seekers, processors, and signal chains, none of which Fernant could reverse engineer at any useful rate, and all of which could be bought from France.

The firm's own dating of the transition is 2005, on the basis of the last programme to be initiated from a foreign article. The characterisation is broadly accepted by external analysts, with the qualification that a firm which spent four decades deriving its architecture from foreign hardware does not become an original designer merely by ceasing to measure new examples.

### Modern period (2005–present)

Fernant's contemporary business is weighted toward radar and fire-control, combat management systems, and guided weapons; ordnance, historically the firm's identity and still its sovereign monopoly, has become the smaller and slower-growing part of the revenue base while remaining strategically indispensable. Admission to the Programme d'Intégration des Technologies à Double Usage in 2012 opened the civil secondary-surveillance and air-traffic radar market, which now provides a meaningful share of sensor-line volume.

The firm's engagement with the Sahel sustainment business has given the Soviet-pattern competence built between 1966 and 1996 an unexpected second life. Partner-state militaries across West Africa operate Soviet-pattern equipment for which the original supply relationships are long dead, and Fernant is one of a very small number of firms anywhere that holds validated manufacturing drawings, materials specifications, and tooling for that equipment — derived, forty years earlier, for entirely different reasons.

## The practice of *rétro-conception*

### Method

Atelier 9's working method was established in the 1960s and remained substantially unchanged for three decades. An incoming article was first characterised non-destructively — photographed, radiographed, weighed, and function-tested where safe — before being disassembled to component level. Each component was measured, its material composition established by the metallurgical laboratory, its surface treatment and heat-treatment history inferred, and its manufacturing route reconstructed from tool marks and grain structure. The output was not a drawing of the component but a drawing of the component *as Fernant could make it*, which was frequently not the same thing.

This last distinction is the origin of the firm's characteristic engineering signature. Where a Soviet component had been made by a process Fernant did not possess, the shop substituted a process it did, and where the substitution introduced uncertainty, it compensated with material. Marzenian reproductions are consistently heavier, thicker in section, and more conservatively toleranced than their originals. The habit outlived its justification: components designed at Fernant in the 2010s, on clean sheets, by engineers who had never reverse engineered anything, still carry margins that no requirement asks for. External assessments describe the firm's products as robust, reliable, maintainable, and roughly fifteen per cent heavier than they need to be.

### The synthesis

The mature Fernant product is a hybrid. Its structure, propulsion, mountings, launch mechanisms, and environmental tolerance derive from the Soviet lineage — designed for conscript maintenance, wide temperature ranges, poor storage, and rough handling. Its sensing, guidance, and signal processing derive from the French lineage — licensed, licence-derived, or bought outright. The two traditions are not merely combined; they are combined in a specific and consistent direction, with the Soviet inheritance always in the parts that must survive and the French inheritance always in the parts that must think.

This has practical consequences that shape the firm's strategic position. The Soviet-derived half of a Fernant weapon is sovereign: the republic can make it, in Marzena, indefinitely, without anyone's permission. The French-derived half is not: seeker focal-plane arrays, high-end signal processors, and certain propellant precursors have no domestic substitute and are sourced principally from France. Every system Fernant fields therefore has a sovereign body and a dependent brain. This is the single most-discussed vulnerability of the firm and, by extension, of the *défense par déni* doctrine that its products underwrite.

### « Ni Moscou ni Paris »

The phrase *ni Moscou ni Paris* — "neither Moscow nor Paris" — originated inside Atelier 9 in the 1980s as a shop slogan and an engineering instruction: take from both, owe neither, and make something that is finally Marzenian. It appeared on internal documents, on a hand-painted board in the shop's assembly bay, and in the firm's induction material for new engineers. It was never intended to be read outside the works.

In 2014 the phrase, and a substantial body of internal engineering documentation contextualising it, was published by *Le Témoin Marzenien* in an investigation of the same title. The material established what had been widely assumed and never demonstrated: that specific fielded Marzenian systems were traceable to identifiable foreign originals, that the firm's internal culture was entirely candid about this, and that the practice had been directed by state instruction from 1966.

The political consequence was smaller than the disclosure warranted. A debate was held in the Assemblée Nationale on 11 June 2014 on an opposition motion calling for an inquiry into the provenance of Marzenian weapons designs. It lasted under three hours. The government's response was that *rétro-conception* was a lawful activity conducted under ministerial instruction in a period of international isolation, that no serving programme depended on it, and that the systems concerned were Marzenian in manufacture, in employment, and in law. The motion was defeated comfortably. The MSM and MNP deputies who had brought it did not return to the subject.

The phrase itself passed into general circulation, where it has been used approvingly — as a compressed statement of the exceptionalist claim that Marzena builds its own things — considerably more often than critically. The firm has not adopted it and has not disowned it. The board in the assembly bay was removed in 2015 and is understood to be in the works' archive.

## Products

Fernant Industries manufactures under OCPM design authority above defined tiers. The firm holds design authority over gun systems, volume fire-control and surveillance radar, combat management systems below the major-combatant tier, and short-range and tactical guided weapons. It holds no design authority over any long-range system, and supplies manufactured subsystems into upper-tier OCPM programmes without visibility of the programmes themselves. Where seeker and electronics integration is concerned, [[Électronique et Systèmes de Défense Marzena]] holds collaborative design roles under OCPM coordination.

### Radar and fire-control

Radar is the firm's largest single revenue line and the least contested part of its business. Fernant occupies the volume and fire-control tier of the Marzenian sensor sector: coastal and maritime surveillance, naval navigation and gun and missile fire-control, ground-based air-surveillance and short-to-medium-range air-defence radar, and, under the PITDU, civil secondary-surveillance and air-traffic radar.

The division of effort with ESDM is by platform type and technology tier rather than by product category. ESDM holds the multifunction active-array sensors, major-combatant combat-system integration, and electronic warfare; Fernant holds the larger volume of fire-control and coastal-surveillance equipment for patrol, amphibious, and shore installations, together with export and civil work. The boundary is imperfect, since Fernant's fire-control competence grew directly out of gun systems and pushes naturally upward into the mid-tier, and the resulting adjacency has been a recurrent source of institutional friction. Both firms describe the friction as an artefact of industrial history rather than designed competition.

### Combat management systems

The **ASTRÉE** family is the firm's fastest-growing line and its clearest case of a competence built without foreign derivation. It originated in the late 1990s as the integration layer required to make Fernant's own guns, radar, and missiles work together on a patrol vessel, and grew into a product in its own right when partner states began buying the vessels.

The scope of the family is deliberately bounded. ASTRÉE is a tactical combat management and fire-control environment for patrol, amphibious, coastal-battery, and short-range air-defence applications. It is not a command-and-control system in the operational or strategic sense: the FAM's SICO/Asterre command environment is the responsibility of the military technical service and its contractors, and Fernant's products interface with it rather than forming part of it. The distinction is maintained rigorously in MDN programme documentation and is occasionally blurred in the firm's export marketing, which has caused comment.

### Guided weapons

Fernant Industries is the designated industrial prime for short-range and tactical guided weapons under OCPM design authority, covering man-portable and short-range air defence, anti-tank weapons, anti-ship and coastal-defence missiles, naval point defence, and guided rockets — the asymmetric sea-denial and air-denial capabilities at the centre of *défense par déni*.

Two products carry the line's reputation.

The **RA-9 Requin** anti-ship and coastal-defence missile is the physical expression of the denial doctrine and the firm's most strategically significant product. Deployed from shore batteries, patrol vessels, and fixed installations, it is the weapon that makes the approaches to the Marzenian coast and to the Canary archipelago expensive to enter. Its airframe and sea-skimming flight profile derive from the French licensed lineage; its motor, launch canister, and environmental design derive from the Soviet. Its export is prohibited above the CCED threshold and has never been authorised.

The **MP-3 Guêpe** man-portable air-defence system is the firm's highest-volume guided weapon and the most widely discussed. Both the Soviet and French man-portable lineages were studied at length at Fernant across three decades, and the Guêpe is derived from neither directly: it is a Marzenian design that carries the lessons of both, with a launch and gas-generator architecture recognisably in the Soviet tradition and a seeker and cooling arrangement recognisably in the French one. It is the most frequently cited example of what the firm means by synthesis, and the most frequently cited example, by European competitors, of what they mean by appropriation.

### Guided rockets and loitering munitions

The **LR-122 Averse** is a direct descendant of the 122 mm Soviet rocket artillery programme begun in 1979 and remains in volume production, with the FAM's residual stock of original-pattern launchers retained alongside it as legacy equipment. The **LR-227 Grêle** is a clean-sheet guided system, designed at Fernant in the 2000s with Western-derived guidance, and is the line's precision capability.

The **VE-2 Faucille** loitering munition is the firm's principal experimental programme and its most uncertain. Development draws on the study of Iranian- and Turkish-pattern systems rather than on the Soviet or French lineages, and the programme's methods are closer to Atelier 9's historical practice than anything else Fernant currently does. The Faucille is in early operational use in limited numbers; it is not a mature capability, its integration into FAM fires is provisional, and the artillery and rocket branches continue to rely on the Averse and Grêle for the effects the Faucille is intended eventually to supplement. Whether the programme becomes a product line or a technology demonstrator that ends is an open question inside the firm.

### Ordnance

Fernant Industries is Marzena's principal dedicated ordnance house and operates the national gun foundry. Its catalogue spans naval main and secondary guns, naval close-in and point-defence gun systems, autocannon, towed and light artillery, mortars, and tank and direct-fire gun systems.

Fernant guns and mountings are fitted to platforms built across the complex. The main and secondary armament of vessels built by both Karmarena Shipyards and [[Groupe SOMAR]] is in large part of Fernant origin, and [[Compagnie Nationale d'Armements Marzeniens|CNAM]] purchases Fernant gun systems for integration into its armoured and wheeled land platforms. This cross-supply makes the firm's ordnance competence a sovereign capability of the complex as a whole and the most explicit case of industrial interdependence within the Marzenian base. The single area of genuine product overlap with CNAM — medium-calibre autocannon appearing on both patrol vessels and armoured vehicles — produces intermittent commercial tension, moderated by the volume of work flowing from Fernant to CNAM as a supplier.

Ordnance is the firm's oldest business, its lowest-margin, and the one it cannot leave. The foundry's status as a single point of supply for the armament of the fleet and a large part of the land force is the firm's most durable claim on the state, and the reason its continued existence is not a commercial question.

The **Fernant works** occupy the original Lyngana basin and the reclaimed ground east of it. The foundry hall of 1911 remains in use. The systems buildings behind it, added between 1978 and 1989 during the isolation-period expansion, house the radar, combat-systems, and missile integration lines and the cleared areas associated with classified programmes.

The **Établissement de Tazrant**, established in 1977 in the Kermane region, holds the firm's energetics, propellant, and warhead operations, dispersed inland for the reasons such sites always are. Its siting was determined by proximity to OCPM's interior test infrastructure and by the isolation-period judgement that the republic's propellant capacity should not sit on a coast that could be blockaded. Motor casting, warhead filling, and static motor firing are conducted at Tazrant; rounds leave the site inert, for final assembly at Juby.

The **Établissement de Juby** is the firm's test and final-assembly establishment inside the perimeter of the Polygone d'Essais Atlantique, OCPM's live-fire range on the southern Atlantic coast of the Sahar region. Everything Fernant builds that flies is proved there. The reason is geographic rather than administrative: the firm's works sit on the northern coastal corridor, whose seaward arc runs into the Strait approaches and the Mediterranean shipping lanes, and no free-flight corridor can be established from it. The Juby corridors run west into open ocean, with downrange telemetry drawn from Canary Islands stations, and can be closed by ordinary notice to mariners and airmen.

The arrangement imposes a permanent logistical cost the firm has borne since the late 1960s. A weapon designed at Fernant is cast at Tazrant, assembled and fired at Juby some 1,100 kilometres to the south, and returns as telemetry — a cycle that adds weeks to every iteration and which the firm's engineers regard as the single largest structural drag on its development pace. Test and evaluation above the firm's own level is conducted by OCPM, at the Centre National d'Évaluation Technique for bench and propulsion work and at the PEA for everything else.

The **Cap Lyngon test facility** at the Fernant works retains what can be done without flying anything: gun proving into instrumented butts, radar and antenna range work, and environmental, shock, and vibration testing. It is the oldest test asset the firm holds and gave the Cap Lyngon class of fleet replenishment ships its name, but no guided weapon has been fired from it since 1968.

### The Tazrant explosion (1994)

On the morning of 3 September 1994, an explosion in the propellant casting hall at Tazrant killed eleven workers and injured over forty. It remains the worst industrial accident in the history of the Marzenian defence-industrial complex.

The commission of inquiry reported in 1996. Its published findings attributed the accident to the ignition of accumulated propellant residue during a casting operation conducted outside procedure, in a building whose ventilation and separation standards had been established in 1977 and not revised. Its more consequential finding was structural: the commission concluded that Tazrant had absorbed a large increase in throughput during the HÉRITAGE-era programme expansion without a corresponding revision of its safety case, and that the site's regulatory oversight — divided between the MDN, the labour inspectorate, and a classified-facility regime that limited both — had not been adequate to identify this.

The site was substantially rebuilt between 1996 and 2000 and its safety case reconstructed. The accident is the origin of the standing local controversy over Tazrant, which concerns groundwater contamination, the adequacy of the exclusion zone, and the compensation settlement reached with the families in 1997 on terms that were not published. The matter recurs in regional politics and has never been resolved to the satisfaction of the communities concerned.

## Workforce and culture

Fernant Industries employs an estimated 9,000 to 11,000 people and is, with its parent, the largest industrial employer of the Fernant coastal economy outside phosphate.

The workforce is sharply two-tiered. A cleared engineering and technical elite — several thousand strong, francophone, credentialled, drawn substantially from the University of Fernant and from French institutions, and holding clearances segmented by programme — occupies the design directorates, the systems lines, and Atelier 9's successor organisation. Beneath it, a much larger semi-skilled and skilled manual workforce staffs the foundry, the machine shops, and the assembly lines, and is drawn substantially from interior migration into the northern coastal corridor.

The two tiers do not mix, do not live in the same districts, and have historically not shared a union. The composition of the firm's workforce maps closely onto the broader tension the lore of Marzenian civic identity has never resolved — a francophone coastal professional stratum and an interior-descended labouring population occupying the same institution on different terms — and the firm is periodically cited in academic work on that subject as its clearest industrial instance. The second generation of interior urban migrants is now well represented in the firm's technical grades and beginning to appear in its engineering ones, which the firm regards as a success and its critics regard as belated.

Unlike the state primes, which carried employment mandates from their founding, Fernant has operated under private cost discipline throughout, retaining specialist competence that cannot easily be rebuilt without the administrative headcount inflation that characterised the state enterprises at comparable scale.

### Rachid Delmas

**Rachid Delmas** (1928–2011) directed Atelier 9 from 1969 to 1994 and is the individual most closely associated with the firm's modern character. Trained as a metallurgist at the Lyngana works before independence, without a university degree and without any formal design education, Delmas ran the shop across its entire productive period — from the naval mountings of the late 1960s, through the isolation-period guided-weapons programmes, to the arrival of the HÉRITAGE cohort whose recruitment he conducted personally.

His reputation inside the firm is close to mythological and rests on a small number of much-repeated stories: that he could establish a component's heat-treatment history by eye and be right; that he refused three offers of a directorship in order to stay in the shop; that he chose the designation "Atelier 9" himself because it was the least interesting thing he could think of. He is credited with the formulation *ni Moscou ni Paris*, though the attribution is uncertain and he denied it.

Delmas retired in 1994, three weeks before the Tazrant explosion, in a coincidence that has attracted more comment than it probably deserves. He gave no interviews, published nothing, and left no papers. The firm's engineering building at Fernant was renamed for him in 2013, two years after his death and one year before the *Ni Moscou ni Paris* disclosures made the name awkward.

## Exports

Fernant's export activity is authorised and bounded by the Commission de Contrôle des Exportations de Défense and directed by MDN policy, within the three-actor arrangement described in the parent article: the MDN and CCED determine what may go where, LDW provides the industrial capacity and commercial presence, and the embassy network and the [[Mission de Coopération Sécuritaire au Sahel|MCSS]] provide the strategic relationship in which the transaction sits.

The governing principle of the export portfolio is generational rather than categorical. What is exported is predominantly the firm's older equipment — superseded gun systems, previous-generation surveillance and fire-control radar, earlier ASTRÉE variants, ammunition, mortars, and autocannon — which sits comfortably below the CCED's capability thresholds and is well matched to the coast-guard and littoral requirements of Marzena's sub-Saharan partners. A patrol vessel from Karmarena Shipyards, a Fernant gun, a Fernant surveillance radar, an ASTRÉE console, and the training and sustainment to operate them constitutes the standard package and the visible face of the group's export business.

A small number of trusted partners receive more. Where a partner state's relationship with Marzena is sufficiently deep — a mature MCSS advisory presence, intelligence liaison, an established sustainment dependency, and a political relationship the MDN assesses as durable — the CCED has authorised current-generation radar, combat management systems, and in a very small number of cases guided weapons at the lower end of the tactical tier. These authorisations are made at ministerial level, case by case, and are not published. They are the instrument by which the MDN differentiates between partners within a framework that is publicly uniform.

Above that, the prohibitions are firm. Complete missile systems and guidance technology above defined thresholds may not be transferred to sub-Saharan partners at all. The RA-9 Requin, the MP-3 Guêpe, and the anti-tank line fall squarely within the prohibited categories, and no export of the Requin has ever been authorised. These are not LDW's decisions to make and the firm does not market the systems concerned.

The Soviet-pattern sustainment business is a distinct and growing activity conducted alongside the export portfolio. Through the DGL-MCO framework, Fernant supplies parts, refurbishment, and technical support for partner-state Soviet-pattern equipment using drawings and tooling derived at Atelier 9 between 1966 and 1996. It is a modest business commercially and a substantial one strategically: it embeds Marzenian standards and supply dependencies in force structures that Russia would otherwise service, in the theatre where the two states are in direct competition.

## Controversies

### Design provenance and intellectual property

The most persistent controversy concerns the legitimacy of the firm's designs. European suppliers — principally French, and in two cases firms whose licensed products Fernant manufactured during the isolation period — have argued in trade forums, in industry association proceedings, and in commercial correspondence that identifiable portions of the Fernant catalogue are derivative of designs the firm neither licensed nor paid for, and that its export of those products competes unlawfully with the originals.

Two disputes reached formal commercial process, in 1987 and in 2003. Both were settled without litigation, on undisclosed terms, and neither produced a public finding. The French state has never made a formal representation on the matter, and the prevailing analytical view is that it will not: the copying was tolerated when it was strategically useful, and raising it now would jeopardise a component supply relationship that gives Paris more leverage over Marzenian capability than any legal remedy would.

The Marzenian position, articulated in the 2014 Assemblée debate and unchanged since, is that *rétro-conception* was lawful under Marzenian law, conducted under ministerial instruction during a period of enforced isolation, and has not been a primary method since 2005; and that the systems currently exported are Marzenian in design, in manufacture, and in law.

### The 2023 Sahel incident

In 2023 a VE-2 Faucille loitering munition was employed against a Marzenian-trained partner-state unit during factional fighting in a Sahel state, in circumstances the MDN has described as the diversion of a single item from a partner's inventory. The incident produced a CCED review of end-use monitoring for the experimental munitions line and a suspension of further transfers pending its conclusion.

It was a single occurrence and has not recurred. Its significance is less as a control failure than as an illustration of the difficulty the Faucille programme presents: a cheap, small, transferable weapon is precisely the item that the CCED's threshold-based regime, designed around missiles and guidance packages, characterises least well.

### Supply-chain composition

The presence of Chinese-sourced components in the lower tiers of the firm's electronics supply chain has appeared in restricted parliamentary committee discussion in the terms applied to the complex generally. Within Fernant, the matter has moved further than public discussion reflects: a programme to identify and remove Chinese-origin components from cleared programmes has been under way since an incident whose nature has not been made public, and which is understood within the firm to have involved a discovery rather than a compromise. The programme is not complete and its cost has not been disclosed.

### Oversight

Opposition members of the Defence Committee have argued that a privately held firm holding the national gun foundry, the tactical guided-weapons prime role, classified facility clearances, and a body of design material of contested provenance is inadequately governed by a golden share extended after the fact. The government's position is that the *action spécifique*, CCED licensing, and OCPM's retention of design authority above the tactical tier constitute equivalent oversight. The argument recurs at each Defence Committee cycle and has not moved.

## Strategic significance and vulnerabilities

Fernant Industries matters to the Republic of Marzena for four reasons. Its foundry is the sovereign source of the armament of the fleet and a large part of the land force, and cannot be replaced from any other domestic supplier. Its guided-weapons lines are the material content of *défense par déni*: the coastal missiles, air-defence systems, and anti-tank weapons that make military action against Marzena expensive without requiring force mass parity with Algeria. Its Soviet-pattern competence, built for reasons that no longer obtain, has become an instrument of Marzenian influence in the Sahel theatre where the republic competes directly with Russia. And it is a substantial component of the skilled and semi-skilled workforce of the northern coastal corridor, with the political weight that carries in a city whose labour force is drawn substantially from interior migration.

Its vulnerabilities are structural and largely unresolved.

The first is the dependent brain. Every system the firm fields carries imported or licence-derived seekers, processors, and signal chains for which no domestic substitute exists and which are sourced principally from France. The sovereign half of a Fernant weapon is the half that survives; the dependent half is the half that works. Forty years of *rétro-conception* produced a firm that can make anything it can measure and cannot make the things that now determine performance.

The second is the ceiling. By design, Fernant holds no design authority over any long-range system and supplies subsystems into OCPM programmes it does not see. This is simultaneously the mechanism of state primacy over the firm and the boundary of its autonomous capability, and it is not a constraint the firm contests: it is understood internally as the structural basis of the relationship that sustains it.

The third is the succession of method. The firm stopped reverse engineering as a primary method around 2005 and has designed at the tactical tier since. Whether that capability is durable — whether a house that spent four decades deriving its architecture from other people's hardware has genuinely become an originating designer, or has merely exhausted its supply of examples — is the central open question about Fernant Industries, and one that the Faucille programme will do more to answer than anything else currently on its books.
