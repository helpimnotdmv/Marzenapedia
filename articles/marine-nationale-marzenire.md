# Marine Nationale Marzenière

The **Marine Nationale Marzenière** is the naval service of the [[Forces Armées Marzenières]] (FAM). It is responsible for maritime defence, the protection of naval communications and national approaches, and the generation of forces for joint operations. Its establishment includes surface ships, submarines, naval aviation, coastal defence and naval infantry. The Navy administers one national fleet through Atlantic and Mediterranean–Strait commands, with principal support centres at Solane and Cap-Nord.

The service has two enduring responsibilities. Submarines, frigates, coastal weapons and surveillance contest an opponent's access to Marzenian waters and reinforcement routes. Amphibious ships, escorts and support vessels allow the Republic to deploy and sustain limited forces beyond its territory. The defence of the Canary Islands connects these roles: the islands require a permanent territorial force, access across the Atlantic approaches and the ability to receive reinforcements from the mainland.

As of 2026, the Navy maintains 43 commissioned principal vessels and approximately 32,000 active personnel. The fleet includes two landing platform docks, sixteen frigates, seven older corvettes, four replenishment and expeditionary support ships, two intelligence vessels, six mine-countermeasure ships and six submarines. Smaller landing, harbour and service craft are accounted for separately. Commissioned strength is greater than the number available for immediate operations because maintenance, qualification and crew cycles remove ships from the operational pool.

The most recent construction programmes renewed much of the frigate and submarine force and were substantially completed by 2025. Their delivery improved capability without eliminating the demands of older vessels, shore infrastructure or technical training. Contemporary debate concerns how much of the commissioned fleet can be kept available, rather than the desirability of another immediate expansion. A further attack submarine remains planned for 2030, while wider surface-force replacement studies have attracted limited funding appetite.

## Missions and maritime posture

The Navy's concept of *maîtrise des approches* concerns the ability to control or deny selected maritime approaches in support of national objectives. It does not assume continuous command of the surrounding Atlantic or Mediterranean. Planning concentrates on the Strait approaches, mainland ports, naval bases, the Canary Islands and the routes connecting them. Ships, aircraft, coastal sensors and missiles contribute different parts of that task.

Maritime denial rests on uncertainty as well as visible presence. An opponent must account for submarines, dispersed coastal weapons and surface forces whose exact readiness or location may not be known. The Navy also maintains the escort and support capacity needed to move its own ships and designated merchant traffic. Denying hostile access and preserving friendly movement are related responsibilities; neither can be reduced to the number of anti-ship weapons in the inventory.

The regional role uses a relatively small expeditionary force. A landing platform dock can embark Army infantry, helicopters, command personnel and medical support, with escorts and replenishment allocated to the mission. Such a group provides options for evacuation, reinforcement, assistance and limited intervention. Its deployment is a national decision and creates demands on the wider fleet, particularly air-defence escorts, technical personnel and support shipping.

The Atlantic and Mediterranean operating areas draw from the same national fleet. Home ports provide crew continuity, workshops and administrative support, but ships are not permanently restricted to one coast. The two maritime commands prepare and administer forces without becoming independent navies. Operational assignments are made through the CEMA and the joint command system.

The service works closely with the Air and Space Force for fixed-wing maritime patrol, larger unmanned aircraft, national air defence and satellite support. It maintains shipborne helicopters and smaller naval drones through its own aviation command. Coastal infantry and missile units extend naval responsibilities ashore, especially in the Canaries, while ordinary civilian policing remains outside their military mission.

## Operational history

### Inherited coastal service, 1952–1972

The Navy was established at independence from a limited coastal service, transferred equipment and personnel trained under French administration. Its initial responsibilities were concentrated around ports, coastal patrol, communications and the maintenance of a national maritime presence. Early officers and technical ratings depended heavily on inherited practices and access to French instruction.

Domestic repair and marine engineering supported the early fleet. The 1963 frontier war reinforced the importance of maritime supply, while the principal fighting remained on land. French naval signalling during that crisis remained a separate contribution rather than a Marzenian fleet action.

By the early 1970s, the Navy had moved beyond a purely coastal patrol establishment. Escorts, an initial submarine capability and improvised transport arrangements provided limited regional reach. These early submarines belonged to predecessor generations, not to the classes currently commissioned. The growth in capability remained greater than the maturity of joint command procedures linking ships, aircraft and embarked troops.

### The Canary campaign and a wider defence perimeter, 1973

*Main article: [[Operation CEINTURE DE FER]].*

During Operation CEINTURE DE FER, from 14 to 24 October 1973, the Navy's central task was controlling the approaches through which Marzenian forces moved and Spanish reinforcements might arrive. Groupe Naval ATLAS combined escorts, patrol craft, submarines, auxiliaries and adapted commercial shipping. The force operated in assigned sectors rather than as a single concentrated battle fleet.

Escorts protected amphibious movements and supply ships, patrol vessels enforced the declared zone, and submarines observed likely approaches. Land-based aviation supplied reconnaissance and other support. Coordination remained dependent on separate reporting channels, radio discipline and the judgement of commanders. Several uncertain contacts demonstrated the difficulty of establishing a common picture without a permanent joint headquarters.

The most consequential naval decisions concerned the possibility of escalation. Spanish forces retained the ability to concentrate against the operation, while Marzenian ships and submarines could not always distinguish an approaching threat from neutral or unrelated movement immediately. National authorities retained decisions whose consequences could expand the campaign. The operation's success rested on Spanish restraint and diplomatic circumstances as well as the readiness to contest an attempted reinforcement.

Three Marzenian personnel were wounded during the campaign, and no fatalities were recorded on either side. The naval history consequently concerns armed pressure, protected movement and controlled risk rather than victory in a fleet battle. Later teaching treated the absence of an engagement as an outcome requiring explanation, not evidence that the deployed weapons and ships had been unnecessary.

Incorporation of the Canaries enlarged the Navy's permanent responsibilities. Island defence, maritime surveillance, naval infantry and reinforcement arrangements became routine requirements. The establishment of a standing coastal infantry regiment followed from the need to protect national positions and maintain the principal infantry force on the islands without assigning a large permanent Army garrison.

### The War of the Sands and fleet development, 1978–2002

During the War of the Sands, 1978–1982, the Navy supported national endurance and helped deter wider escalation. It protected maritime supply, maintained readiness around the approaches and prepared for contingencies that might extend the conflict beyond its land front. The service did not fight a separate decisive naval campaign. Its contribution depended on keeping maritime options available while the Army absorbed the principal combat commitment.

During the 1980s and 1990s, domestic construction produced patrol combatants, mine-countermeasure vessels, support ships and successive submarine improvements. The current older corvettes and remaining *Raison*-class submarine derive from this period. Permanent joint commands later gave the Navy a clearer framework for assigning maritime forces to national campaigns without surrendering service responsibility for ships and crews.

The amphibious programme produced **TCD Lévane**, commissioned in 1996, and **TCD Solane**, commissioned in 2002. Both were built through LDW's shipbuilding organisation at Fernant. The class provided a docking well, helicopter facilities, troop accommodation, medical spaces and an embarked headquarters. These were landing platform docks, not full-length flight-deck helicopter assault ships. Their command and projection functions explain the broader terminology sometimes used in earlier descriptions of the programme.

### Operation ESTUAIRE, 2003

*Main article: [[Operation ESTUAIRE (2003)]].*

In June 2003, Marzena deployed an amphibious group led by *Lévane* to the West African littoral under **Operation ESTUAIRE**. Its operating area included the Liberian approaches and ports farther east in the Gulf of Guinea. The deployment took place during the final major fighting of the Second Liberian Civil War and the continuing Ivorian crisis following the September 2002 rebellion and coup attempt.

Contre-amiral **Laurent Delmas** commanded the embarked joint headquarters. Capitaine de vaisseau **Claire Veyrac** commanded *Lévane*, retaining responsibility for the ship and its safe operation. The distinction became an important example in subsequent instruction: the force commander could direct the campaign, while the ship's captain remained responsible for the platform on which much of that command depended.

The group included Army marine infantry, a mixed helicopter detachment, medical personnel, two escorts and a replenishment ship. The escorts belonged to the preceding surface generation rather than the frigate classes delivered during the later renewal programmes. The replenishment ship **Cap Lyngon**, commissioned in 1999, supported the movement and remained part of the serving fleet afterward. *Solane* was still establishing its full operational cycle and was not counted as a second deployed amphibious ship throughout the mission.

The initial priority was non-combatant evacuation readiness: maintaining transport, reception, medical and protection capacity while diplomatic posts identified those who might require assistance. As the situation developed, the force conducted evacuation movements and shifted personnel, stores and medical support between agreed points. The mission's significance lay in maintaining that capacity over time, rather than conducting a single large landing.

Tasks changed during the deployment. Expeditionary logistics and partner support became more prominent as evacuation requirements fluctuated. Escorts protected designated movements and discouraged opportunistic interference with the group's shipping. Maritime-security activity concerned immediate threats around the operation; it did not amount to a permanent regional anti-piracy campaign. Neither shipboard presence nor an evacuation mandate conferred general authority over local ports and coastal waters.

The group coordinated access and movements with regional authorities and other forces operating in the area. It retained a national chain of command while supporting agreed practical tasks. Medical teams provided casualty care and, when reception capacity permitted, assistance to local services. Diplomats used these activities to demonstrate Marzena's usefulness as a partner, while medical personnel retained responsibility for treatment priorities. The embarked facilities supplemented rather than replaced local hospitals or the larger relief effort.

Following Liberia's August peace settlement, the balance shifted further toward support, medical activity and the orderly reduction of the force. The deployment concluded in November 2003 after successive tasks were completed or transferred. It did not resolve either country's political crisis. Its naval significance was the ability to maintain a changing joint mission from an afloat headquarters, with the ship, landing force, aviation and support chain operating under a defined mandate.

Lessons from ESTUAIRE influenced embarked command arrangements, evacuation preparation and the later acquisition of dedicated expeditionary support vessels. Accommodation, helicopter maintenance, medical reception and stores had repeatedly competed for the same space and personnel.

### Renewal and consolidation, 2004–2026

The next major investment wave concentrated on surface escorts and submarines. Frigate deliveries were grouped in overlapping programmes from 2010 to 2024, while the *Vigilance* and *Indépendance* submarines entered service between 2012 and 2025. Completion of the principal deliveries left the Navy with a substantially renewed core but a demanding transition in qualifications, maintenance and technical support.

The *Fernant* air-defence frigates were delivered in two batches, the later ships incorporating a revised design. The *Cap-Nord* class supplied substantial surface-warfare and command capability, while the more numerous *Port-Sahar* frigates took on general and anti-submarine escort duties. Older corvettes continued to provide secondary presence as these programmes absorbed funding and experienced crews.

By 2026, the principal problem had shifted from taking delivery of new hulls to sustaining the force already acquired. Technical retention, maintenance scheduling and the preparation of relief crews determine how much of the commissioned fleet is available. A further attack submarine is planned for 2030 as a follow-on to the current family, while proposals for another large surface construction programme remain at the study stage.

## Command and organisation

### National authority and geographic administration

The **Chef d'état-major de la Marine** heads the service from the État-major de la Marine in Lévane. The naval staff manages personnel, force preparation, technical standards, resources and service development. The President determines national military objectives, while the CEMA directs operational assignments through the FAM's joint structure. The Navy generates ships, crews and supporting units for those assignments.

The **Commandement Maritime Atlantique**, based at Solane, administers most Atlantic and expeditionary surface forces. The **Commandement Maritime Méditerranée–Détroit**, based at Cap-Nord, administers forces oriented toward the Mediterranean and Strait approaches. These commands are responsible for preparation, local support and administrative continuity. Their boundaries do not prevent ships from receiving assignments elsewhere.

The two commands do not create separate national fleets or independent foreign-policy authorities. A ship's home port and permanent squadron identify who prepares and supports it; operational orders identify who commands its current mission. A mixed group can therefore draw ships from both administrative commands without transferring their permanent ownership or creating duplicate establishment entries.

Submarines, naval aviation and shore forces have specialised service commands because their preparation and technical responsibilities extend beyond ordinary surface-squadron administration. They retain permanent relationships with the main bases. These functional organisations coexist with geographic administration without placing every ship under two competing routine chains.

### Permanent mixed surface squadrons

Surface ships belong to mixed squadrons organised around missions rather than class alone. Permanent groupings support training, command familiarity and administrative preparation. They are not fixed deployment formations: a squadron may supply several ships to one task group, retain others in maintenance and provide a ship to an operation led by another headquarters.

The 2026 establishment divides the sixteen frigates between escort, denial and presence squadrons. Amphibious ships and dedicated expeditionary support vessels share a permanent organisation. Replenishment, intelligence and mine-countermeasure ships retain smaller specialist groupings because their services are allocated across the fleet.

Squadron staffs retain responsibility for standards and preparation during refits and withdrawals. A commander assembling a task group must verify the availability of individual ships rather than assume that the published squadron is ready as a whole.

### Joint commands and embarked areas of responsibility

COI Atlantique–Canaries is the principal standing joint framework for Atlantic and island operations. Naval forces also support other COIs and national missions. The geographic naval commands provide service administration; the COIs exercise the joint operational authority assigned by the CEMA. Similar geographic names do not make them interchangeable institutions.

A TCD operating beyond an established joint theatre can embark a **commandement opérationnel interarmées expéditionnaire**. Under a CEMA directive, the headquarters receives a defined expeditionary area of responsibility, assigned forces and a mission. The ship then serves as the principal command platform for that area. Its practical reach depends on communications, supporting ships, aviation and any forces ashore.

This arrangement is sometimes described as the amphibious ship carrying its own command area. The authority belongs to the embarked joint headquarters under the national mandate, not automatically to the ship wherever it sails. The area has specified responsibilities and limits and can be changed or terminated by the CEMA. It does not constitute a permanent naval jurisdiction over foreign territory or every sea through which the ship passes.

The ship's captain remains responsible for the vessel, navigation, engineering and safety. The force commander directs assigned operations. Decisions affecting what the platform can safely support require coordination between them, particularly when medical reception, aviation, troop accommodation and command facilities are all heavily used. ESTUAIRE remains a frequently cited example of this distinction.

On entry into an existing COI's operational area, command relationships are specified before activities begin. The ship may become a subordinate command node, provide an attached task group or retain a separately authorised mission with defined coordination. An amphibious headquarters does not displace the standing theatre commander merely because it has arrived with a landing force.

### Submarine organisation

The **Commandement des Forces sous-marines** administers the **1re Escadrille sous-marine**, comprising the three *Vigilance* boats and the remaining *Raison*, and the **2e Escadrille sous-marine**, comprising the two *Indépendance* cruise-missile boats. Both use Cap-Nord as their principal support and qualification centre. Atlantic missions draw on the same force rather than an independent Atlantic submarine fleet.

The separate second squadron reflects the *Indépendance* boats' distinct mission preparation, command links and technical requirements. It does not make them a separate armed service. Conventional strategic strike tasking passes through the reserved national chain. Routine administration, maintenance and the preparation of crews remain Navy responsibilities.

DRET and CRMO provide intelligence support according to mission and classification. Submarine commanders receive the information needed for their task through authorised channels; a collection requirement does not transfer the submarine outside its military operational chain. Public descriptions identify classes and general roles without publishing patrol areas, mission schedules or weapon loads.

Selected additional crews support the modern submarines. They allow relief, training and the preparation of a later deployment without requiring a hull to wait for every member of its previous crew to complete the same leave cycle. The system is selective rather than a complete two-crew arrangement for every boat. Technical qualifications, maintenance and experience still limit utilisation.

### Naval shore forces and the Canaries

The **Commandement des Forces navales à terre** administers naval infantry, base-protection units and coastal missile forces. It gives the Navy a common structure for land-based military responsibilities while retaining different operational roles within it. The command does not absorb the Army's marine brigade or acquire ordinary civilian police powers.

The **Régiment d'infanterie côtière des Canaries** is the principal standing infantry and territorial military formation on the islands. Its headquarters is at Gando, with two permanent battalions and regimental supporting companies. The 1st Battalion is centred on Gran Canaria and provides detachments to western island positions. The 2nd is centred on Lanzarote and supports eastern island responsibilities. These centres are administrative concentrations, not claims that all personnel remain at one installation.

The regiment protects naval facilities, coastal positions, important access points and the reception of reinforcements. Its territorial role is broader than guarding a single naval base. It maintains knowledge of the islands' military infrastructure and supplies the standing infantry command through which additional naval detachments can be organised. Larger land-combat contingencies still require Army and other national reinforcement.

The regiment's establishment is approximately 1,500–1,800, including its battalions, communications, transport, engineers, medical support and headquarters. Supporting companies are designed for territorial service rather than a large independent amphibious assault. The regiment is therefore distinct from both Army expeditionary marine infantry and the classified maritime special-operations community.

Mainland naval protection is supplied by the **Bataillon de protection navale de Solane** and **Bataillon de protection navale de Cap-Nord**, with smaller detachments at other naval installations. They sit within the same shore-force command. Their duties include military installation protection and preparation for emergency reinforcement, with base authorities responsible for integrating routine support and access arrangements.

The **Groupement mobile de défense côtière** administers the national pool of mobile coastal anti-ship units. A dedicated Canary detachment remains permanently assigned to island defence, while additional units can reinforce the islands or mainland approaches. Smaller standing mainland detachments maintain local preparation without dividing the entire missile inventory into immovable regional establishments.

Coastal missile units share administration with naval infantry but have separate technical and operational responsibilities. Missile employment is coordinated through the relevant joint command and recognised maritime and air pictures. Infantry protects sites and movement where assigned; it does not independently acquire authority to select and engage maritime targets. Mobility is intended to preserve options and survivability, not to remove the need for preparation and support.

### Naval aviation and specialist boundaries

The **Commandement de l'Aéronavale** administers shipborne helicopters, smaller naval unmanned systems, aviation training and technical support. Its principal centre is at Solane, with detachments supporting Cap-Nord and Gando. Exact aircraft types are treated in specialist programme descriptions; the service-level establishment distinguishes maritime helicopters, utility and support helicopters, and naval drone detachments.

Maritime helicopters support anti-submarine work, surveillance, liaison and the ship's ability to examine contacts beyond its immediate horizon. Utility aircraft support movement, medical evacuation and ship-to-shore tasks. TCD deployments can embark a mixed group of transport, attack and maritime helicopters, but many transport and attack aircraft are supplied by the Army. Their presence aboard a Navy ship does not transfer their permanent establishment to naval aviation.

Fixed-wing maritime patrol and large maritime drones remain operated and maintained by the Air and Space Force. Naval or joint commanders receive operational tasking authority when those aircraft are assigned to a mission. This arrangement avoids duplicating substantial fixed-wing fleets while preserving the requirement for aircrews to train closely with ships and maritime headquarters.

The Navy recruits and supports personnel for the maritime elements of CRMO/CSOF. The detailed structure is classified and is not included as an additional naval infantry regiment in the public establishment. DRET retains strategic intelligence responsibilities; CRMO supports operational military requirements. Navy participation in either system does not merge their mandates or make every intelligence-linked sailor a special operator.

### Bases, maintenance and common support

Support is centred on the principal naval bases. **Solane** provides Atlantic fleet services, amphibious loading, expeditionary preparation and major industrial connections. **Cap-Nord** provides Mediterranean services, submarine support and specialist maintenance access. **Gando** supplies forward command, aviation, naval infantry and reception support in the Canaries without duplicating the full industrial capacity of a mainland complex.

Base commands manage workshops, stores, harbour services, accommodation and routine local support. The naval staff establishes common technical standards and allocates major maintenance priorities. Industrial yards conduct construction and contracted repair according to programme arrangements. A ship entering a private or mixed-ownership yard remains under military administration even when most physical work is performed by civilian specialists.

CIL sets national stock and strategic movement priorities and coordinates cross-service requirements. Naval bases and replenishment organisations translate those priorities into the stores, fuel, parts and technical support required by ships. Naval supply is therefore part of joint logistics without being administered as though a deployed ship were an Army convoy or depot.

CTI provides common communications and cryptographic standards. Navy specialists operate and maintain the naval systems built to those standards, including the links through which SICO receives selected force information. Combat-management, air-defence and submarine systems retain functions that do not depend on the general headquarters picture being continuously available.

Medical support combines shipboard capability, the CMI and civilian reception arrangements. The Navy supplies ordinary embarked medical personnel and facilities; larger deployments receive additional qualified teams. TCD medical spaces can support casualty reception and limited surgery when appropriately staffed. They are not permanently equivalent to a fully staffed hospital ship simply because the physical compartments exist.

### Landing platform docks

**TCD Lévane** and **TCD Solane** provide the Navy's principal amphibious capacity. Their formal French designation is *transport de chalands de débarquement*, corresponding here to landing platform dock. Each combines a docking well for landing craft, vehicle and troop spaces, a helicopter operating deck, hangar support, medical compartments and facilities for an embarked headquarters.

The design places the docking and transport function at its centre. The ships have an aft aviation deck rather than a continuous full-length flight deck. They can support a mixed helicopter detachment within the available deck, hangar and maintenance capacity. Embarking transport, attack and maritime aircraft together requires a mission-specific plan; it does not give the ship the simultaneous capacity of several specialised aviation platforms.

The usual landing force is a reinforced battalion drawn from the Army's marine brigade. Engineers, communications, medical and logistical personnel are added according to the task. Troop accommodation and vehicle spaces can also be used for evacuation reception or relief stores, but changing their use affects what else can be embarked. The Navy's own Canary coastal regiment is not the default landing force for these deployments.

The ships provide a useful command platform because they can accommodate a joint staff alongside the transport and support functions that staff needs. Their command role remains constrained by platform availability and communications. A headquarters can direct assets beyond the ship's immediate vicinity, but its sustained effectiveness depends on national links, local liaison and a support chain that reaches the operational area.

*Lévane* entered service in 1996 and established the practical basis of the expeditionary role through training and ESTUAIRE. Its 2016–2018 refit renewed important ship and support systems. *Solane*, commissioned in 2002, incorporated lessons from the first ship and underwent its major refit during 2022–2024. These dates preserve a two-ship programme separated by several years rather than a single simultaneous delivery.

Normally one TCD can be generated for an operation while the other is undergoing maintenance, preparing or recovering. Exceptional overlap is possible, but planning does not assume that both will remain deployed indefinitely. No third ship has been ordered. Studies of eventual replacement remain separate from the immediate requirement to maintain the existing pair.

### Frigate force

The **Cap-Nord class** consists of four large frigates with substantial anti-ship weapons, air defence and command facilities. They provide the Navy with surface combatants able to lead mixed groups and contribute materially to a contested maritime operation. Their classification as frigates reflects their place in Marzenian organisation; the ships are not described as a separate destroyer force.

Their principal emphasis is surface warfare combined with strong air defence. Anti-submarine equipment remains part of the fit, but the class does not displace the more specialised escort work of other ships. Their command spaces allow a flag staff to lead a naval task group when the operation does not require the larger embarked joint establishment carried by a TCD.

The **Fernant class** provides the strongest dedicated area-air-defence capability in the surface fleet. The ships contribute surveillance, engagement capacity and coordination for the defence of several vessels rather than only their own hull. They are particularly valuable when an amphibious ship, replenishment vessel or important reinforcement movement needs protection.

The first three ships entered service during 2010–2014. The two delivered in 2021 and 2023 belong to a revised construction batch with changes to power, electronic integration and system support. Modernisation of the first batch began in 2024. It improves commonality but does not make the earlier hulls physically identical to the later pair. Availability planning accounts for refits and differences in qualification rather than presenting all five ships as interchangeable on every task.

The **Port-Sahar class** supplies seven general and anti-submarine escorts. These ships are intended to perform many of the patrol, escort and reinforcement tasks for which the larger frigates would be costly to use routinely. They carry sensors, weapons and helicopter facilities suited to anti-submarine escort, alongside general surface capability.

Their air defence is principally for self-protection and close escort circumstances. A *Port-Sahar* assigned to an amphibious group does not replace the area-defence contribution of a *Fernant*. The division allows the Navy to sustain a larger number of useful escorts without fitting every hull with the most demanding national air-defence suite.

The sixteen frigates are distributed among four mixed squadrons. Their permanent distribution supports training across complementary capabilities, while operational groups can be assembled differently. Frigate numbers have increased the Navy's options, but each new hull also requires a qualified engineering department, combat-system maintainers and an established shore support path. The completion of construction therefore marks the beginning of a long operating commitment rather than the end of a programme's demands.

### Corvettes and secondary presence

The three remaining **Détroit-class** corvettes provide a reduced coastal strike and surveillance force. Their original design and smaller hulls impose limits on endurance, accommodation and later upgrades. Refurbishment preserved selected functions, but did not turn them into equivalents of the newer frigates. Their remaining employment concentrates on secondary duties for which their cost and existing crews remain useful.

The four commissioned **Alizé-class** corvettes conduct routine presence, coastal surveillance and training. They help avoid assigning a large frigate to every visible naval task. Their ageing machinery and varying remaining life require more individual scheduling than a simple four-ship class total suggests. Some can sustain a useful programme while another needs work disproportionate to its limited future service.

Withdrawals proceed gradually. Retiring a ship releases a crew and maintenance demand, but also removes a hull from routine presence. The Navy has not funded a new offshore-patrol class to replace these functions as a separate programme. It instead manages the remaining ships and the allocation of light frigates while considering wider surface-force options.

Withdrawn hulls held for parts or disposal are excluded from commissioned strength. They are not treated as an immediately available combat reserve. Recovering a ship after formal withdrawal would require a separate decision, technical work and a crew; its continued physical presence in a basin does not preserve the capability it once represented.

### Submarines

The six-boat submarine force includes three modern attack boats, one older attack boat and two conventional strike submarines. Four are normally operationally assigned, while maintenance, conversion and work-up affect the remainder. Two or three are generally assessed as available for prompt deployment. Those figures fluctuate and are not a statement that the same boats are always available.

The **Vigilance class** conducts maritime denial, reconnaissance and selected wider Atlantic patrols. All three are diesel-electric boats. The first, **Lanzarote**, entered service in 2012 and completed a submerged-endurance conversion in 2024. **La Palma**, commissioned in 2016, began the corresponding work in 2025. **El Hierro**, commissioned in 2020, incorporated the enhanced arrangement during construction.

The mixed modernisation state affects maintenance and qualification as well as endurance. Crews and shore personnel must understand the particular boat to which they are assigned. The Navy has retained common elements where practical but does not assume that the conversion eliminates every difference between the early and later hulls.

The **Indépendance class** is an enlarged derivative of the attack-submarine design family. **Grande Canarie** entered service in 2021 and **Ténériffe** in 2025. Both use diesel-electric propulsion with enhanced submerged endurance and carry conventional cruise-missile capability alongside their submarine warfare functions. They are neither nuclear-powered boats nor an operational nuclear-armed force.

The design provides space and systems for the strike role, with related increases in support and training requirements. Its value lies in providing a covert conventional option under national command. It does not promise continuous distant presence by both boats. The newer submarine's commissioning is followed by further qualification and the establishment of a mature maintenance and crew cycle.

The separate 2nd Submarine Squadron supports these requirements. Strategic mission decisions remain reserved through the CEMA chain, while the Navy administers the boats and crews. The specialist relationship does not remove them from ordinary naval obligations concerning safety, engineering assurance and command responsibility.

**Kermane**, commissioned in 1989, is the last serving **Raison-class** submarine. Its remaining functions are training, secondary patrol and limited reserve availability. It provides useful experience and absorbs tasks that need not consume a newer boat, but requires careful assessment of the value of further maintenance. Its planned withdrawal remains no later than 2031.

A further attack submarine, provisionally named **La Gomera**, is planned to commission in 2030. It is a follow-on to the modern attack-boat family and is intended to replace *Kermane*, preserving four attack boats alongside the two conventional strike submarines. It is discussed here as a planned programme and is not included in the commissioned-vessel table or the six-boat serving total.

### Replenishment and expeditionary support

The **Cap Lyngon class** comprises two fleet replenishment ships, commissioned in 1999 and 2006. They provide fuel and stores support to deployed naval groups and reduce dependence on repeated port calls. The lead ship's service in ESTUAIRE demonstrated the importance of replenishment to a mission whose tasks and location changed over several months.

Major refits in 2018 and 2022 addressed the ships' continued service requirements. Their support role remains different from that of the **Baie de Solane class**, whose two ships entered service in 2008 and 2012. The latter carry expeditionary stores, vehicles, workshops and other resources for forces operating ashore or from an amphibious group.

The expeditionary support ships relieve TCDs of some cargo and sustained logistical tasks. They do not possess the same landing, aviation and joint command arrangement and are not counted as two additional amphibious assault platforms. The two classes together allow the Navy to distinguish sustaining the fleet at sea from sustaining the wider mission.

Four large support vessels do not guarantee that every naval commitment can have a dedicated auxiliary. Maintenance and concurrent operations require allocation between groups. Charter and requisition arrangements can provide additional cargo capacity, but civilian shipping does not automatically reproduce a naval replenishment ship's functions or a military support crew's preparation.

### Intelligence and mine warfare

The two **Veille-class** ships are purpose-built collection vessels rather than improvised conversions. They provide endurance, working space and technical support for embarked collection teams. The Navy operates the ships and maintains their marine systems. Collection equipment is renewed through programmes whose schedules need not coincide with the hull's major refits.

Tasking depends on the mission. DRET directs strategic collection requirements, while CRMO coordinates military operational requirements and the support required by a campaign. A mission order identifies the responsible tasking authority, distribution arrangements and naval operational relationship. The two agencies do not independently issue conflicting daily directions to the ship merely because both have an interest in its collected information.

The six **Passe-class** mine-countermeasure vessels form an older and less prominent capability. Their duties include investigation and clearance of suspected threats around routes, ports and operating areas. The class has received uneven refurbishment and normally generates fewer operationally available ships than its commissioned total suggests.

Mine warfare has not secured full institutional recognition as an indispensable element of the wider fleet plan. Some planners assume that threats can be monitored, routes changed or assistance obtained, leaving dedicated clearance capability behind more visible programmes. This outlook affects training and renewal as well as money. The Navy consequently retains a capability that is useful but receives less sustained attention than the ports and reinforcement routes it is meant to support.

No comprehensive replacement has been approved. The existing flotilla continues operating while proposals compete with the costs of newer frigates, submarines and amphibious support. Its small scale also limits the number of officers and technical personnel able to represent its requirements in broader planning.

### Minor craft and construction arrangements

Landing craft, harbour tugs, service launches, diving boats and smaller naval drones are described collectively at service level. TCD landing craft belong to the amphibious support system; harbour and maintenance vessels belong to their bases. Their availability matters to embarkation, docking and local support, but they are not included as additional principal warships in the fleet total.

Naval construction is allocated programme by programme between SOMAR, LDW's shipbuilding organisation and specialist suppliers. Major contracts identify a lead integrator while allowing hull sections, machinery work, electronics and subsequent maintenance to be shared. OCPM retains authority over protected technical requirements and sensitive integration, with ESDM and other firms contributing defined systems.

SOMAR remains prominent in the modern frigate and submarine programmes. LDW's construction of the TCDs is an established credential, and its yards undertake support, refurbishment and other naval work. These histories create strong specialisations without establishing a permanent legal monopoly over every future vessel category. Capacity, existing facilities and programme decisions determine the division of work.

The latest procurement wave has only recently completed its principal surface and submarine deliveries. Funding and industrial attention now concentrate on completing qualifications, sustaining production knowledge and supporting the ships already commissioned. Surface replacement studies continue, but there is little appetite for opening another project on the scale of the recently delivered fleet before its long-term operating costs are clearer.

## Personnel and ranks

### Establishment and accounting

The Navy's active establishment is approximately **32,000**. This includes ships' crews, naval aviation, shore forces, military base personnel, schools, headquarters and the relief and qualification establishments needed to sustain the fleet. It excludes civilian employees of shipyards and contractors, Army personnel embarked as a landing force, and other-service detachments counted on their own establishment.

The figure is a service-wide planning estimate rather than the number of sailors at sea. A submarine crew, coastal infantry battalion, aviation maintenance section and naval training school all belong to the Navy but contribute differently to immediate deployment. Counting only primary ships' companies would omit much of the work needed to make those companies effective.

| Personnel component | Approximate active allocation | Scope |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Ships, boats, afloat staffs and seagoing relief arrangements | 6,500 | Principal ships' companies, minor craft, selected additional crews and afloat command personnel |
| Naval shore forces | 4,500 | Canary regiment, mainland protection, coastal missiles and their own support |
| Naval aviation | 2,500 | Navy aircrews, aircraft maintenance, operating detachments and aviation support |
| Base services, military maintenance and naval stores | 9,500 | Military personnel sustaining mainland and forward facilities; civilian industrial labour excluded |
| Common training, qualification pipeline and reserve cadres | 6,000 | Recruit and specialist instruction, supervised preparation and permanent reserve administration |
| Naval headquarters and central technical and service administration | 3,000 | Staff, technical direction, service personnel management and common administration |
| **Total** | **Circa 32,000** | **Rounded planning allocations, counted once by primary establishment** |

These allocations describe where posts are funded. A person temporarily embarked from a school or technical establishment is not added to the total a second time. Aviation-specific instructors remain in the aviation component where their post belongs; the common training line does not count every person currently attending any course. Attached joint medical or communications teams are likewise accounted for through their permanent establishment assignment.

The shore-force allocation gives the Canary regiment a prominent but proportionate position. Its 1,500–1,800 personnel form the largest single standing infantry organisation in the archipelago, but the regiment is not the whole Navy presence there. Aviation, base support, missile detachments and the forward joint headquarters have separate functions and accounting.

### Ships' companies and rotating crews

Most surface combatants have one principal ship's company. The crew maintains the knowledge of its own hull, systems and recurring faults that cannot be reproduced instantly by substituting personnel with nominally equivalent qualifications. Leave and individual replacements are arranged around deployment, maintenance and training cycles.

Additional crew arrangements are concentrated where they provide a practical return. The *Vigilance* force maintains an additional qualified crew pool across its three boats, with assignments requiring the appropriate boat-specific preparation. The older *Raison* retains its own more limited crew and training arrangement. The modern boats' shared design does not eliminate the differences introduced by endurance conversions and individual refits.

The *Indépendance* squadron is developing two crews for each boat. The arrangement is mature on *Grande Canarie*, while *Ténériffe* is still completing qualification of its second crew. This distinction matters to the practical use of a submarine commissioned only in 2025. An authorised second crew becomes useful only as its watchkeepers, engineers and command team complete the necessary supervised service.

High-demand replenishment ships also use selected rotating personnel and relief detachments. These arrangements preserve continuity of service while retaining experienced personnel in critical positions. They do not amount to two complete interchangeable crews on every auxiliary, and the TCDs do not assume continuous deployment through unrestricted whole-crew replacement.

The Navy evaluates crew rotation together with the maintenance cycle. Additional sailors cannot make a ship available while its machinery or safety systems require substantial work. Conversely, a ship emerging from refit may still need a period of crew preparation before it can accept a demanding assignment. Readiness certification addresses the vessel, personnel and expected mission together.

### Simplified naval ranks

The Navy adopted simplified naval equivalents during the coordinated FAM personnel reform accompanying SICO's rollout in the late 2010s. The same administrative objective applied across services: rank, appointment and qualification needed to be recorded consistently. The Navy retained maritime titles rather than adopting the Army's names for corresponding grades.

Implementation encountered resistance because older petty-officer grades were closely associated with trade progression, mess arrangements and authority aboard particular ships. Officers and senior ratings also objected where they believed a common personnel scheme understated the responsibility of a qualified watchkeeper or technical supervisor. Transition arrangements preserved recognised qualifications and relevant service entitlements while consolidating substantive grades.

The resulting hierarchy distinguishes **matelots and quartiers-maîtres**, **officiers mariniers**, commissioned officers and flag officers. Professional certificates remain important but do not create additional ranks. A sailor's qualification to stand a particular watch is recorded separately from grade. This allows experience and technical responsibility to remain visible without reconstructing the full former hierarchy in informal titles.

The two highest designations are appointment-linked distinctions. A ship's *commandant* holds a command appointment rather than a substantive rank of that name. Command also requires the appropriate professional qualifications.

Typical appointments vary with vessel size, technical demands and the particular post. Submarine, aviation or engineering qualifications may be essential alongside the grade requirement.

### Entry, specialisation and supervised qualification

Recruit preparation separates into specialities relatively early. All entrants receive essential instruction in naval discipline, sea safety and common service obligations, but deck, engineering, electronics, aviation and shore-force personnel then follow different routes. The Navy does not maintain a long undifferentiated training period before determining the trade for which a recruit is being prepared.

The **École navale de Solane** provides the principal operational officer route. The **École technique navale de Cap-Nord** prepares engineering and technical officers through a distinct initial programme. Both routes lead into the same commissioned hierarchy. Subsequent eligibility for command or senior technical appointments depends on the required experience and qualification, not merely on the institution attended at entry.

Ratings attend trade schools and then complete supervised work within their receiving organisation. Petty-officer development combines instruction with assessed performance in watches, workshops and supervisory appointments. The Navy places particular importance on whether a person can perform reliably within the ship's actual team, rather than only demonstrate knowledge in a school environment.

Submarine qualification is especially dependent on extended supervised service. Personnel must learn their own department and the wider boat sufficiently to contribute to safe operation in a confined and technically interdependent environment. Qualification involves assessments over time, not a single school certificate. Conversion to another design or materially changed standard requires additional preparation.

Surface watchkeeping, engineering certification and aviation-related duties follow the same general distinction between initial instruction and permission to act independently. Senior personnel assess candidates during ordinary service and exercises. The process consumes qualified supervisory time, which makes the retention of experienced instructors and watchkeepers a direct determinant of future fleet availability.

Naval infantry and coastal missile specialists receive the qualifications appropriate to their military tasks through their shore-force organisation and supporting schools. Their common Navy membership does not require them to follow the same practical route as a submarine engineer. Conversely, a sailor's seagoing experience does not automatically qualify that person for territorial infantry duties.

The limited national-service programme is filled by volunteers in practice. Naval participants normally enter roles for which a short period of preparation can be useful, such as selected base and support functions. It is not the principal source of qualified combatant crews. Entry into professional sea service requires the longer occupational and supervised preparation appropriate to the post.

### Technical retention and reserves

The main personnel constraint is the retention of experienced technical specialists. Marine engineering, electrical systems, electronics and naval aviation compete with civilian employers for people whose qualifications have taken years to develop. Losing a senior maintainer can affect not only the current ship but also the training of several replacements.

The Navy uses shore appointments, instructional service and selected relief arrangements to retain experience across a career. A period ashore can preserve a sailor's service while supporting the schools and workshops that the fleet requires. Such measures do not remove private-sector competition, particularly after a major construction programme has increased demand for many of the same skills across military and civilian organisations.

Reserves principally reinforce bases, logistics, repair and territorial naval tasks. The Navy also maintains connections with merchant masters, engineers, port specialists and other civilian maritime professionals. Their knowledge is valuable for transport planning and mobilisation, but civilian expertise does not automatically confer qualification for a warship appointment.

Some specialists hold reserve status and undertake the relevant training. Others support defence through civilian contracts or transport arrangements while retaining civilian employment and responsibilities. Mobilising a merchant vessel and activating a reserve naval crew are separate actions. Plans identify the authority, personnel and technical preparation required rather than treating the merchant marine as an unrecorded reserve fleet.

## Fleet availability and contemporary position

The Navy has a substantially renewed frigate and submarine core, two amphibious command platforms and the support structure needed for selective regional operations. Geographic administration and mixed mission squadrons allow the same national fleet to prepare for Atlantic, Mediterranean and expeditionary requirements. The Canary coastal regiment and mobile missile organisation provide a standing land-based contribution to maritime and territorial defence.

Its principal constraint is the relationship between fleet size and availability. Forty-three commissioned principal vessels do not yield forty-three simultaneous operational assignments. Refit, technical qualification, crew relief, ammunition and the condition of supporting facilities all affect what can be generated. Some small and older ships can remain listed with modest readiness, while a single missing technical qualification can delay a much newer combatant.

Planning therefore evaluates the group needed for a mission rather than the availability of its flagship alone. A TCD may be ready while a suitable escort, replenishment ship or aviation detachment is committed elsewhere. A submarine may complete maintenance before its crew has finished qualification. A port may receive reinforcements but still depend on an ageing mine-countermeasure capability for a particular contingency.

The completion of the latest construction wave has increased these coordination requirements. New ships improve the range and quality of available options, but their long-term value depends on sustained technical support and qualified crews. Existing programmes and the planned 2030 attack submarine continue; a further large surface project remains a study rather than an assumed future fleet.

The service consequently preserves both of its enduring responsibilities. Maritime denial and escort protect the national approaches and reinforcement routes, while amphibious and support forces make limited regional action possible. The question in current planning is how often, for how long and in what combinations those capabilities can be kept available within the personnel and maintenance establishment the Republic is prepared to sustain.
