# Armée de Terre

The **Armée de Terre** is the land service of the [[Forces Armées Marzenières]] (FAM). Established at independence in 1952, it is responsible for continental ground defence, the generation of forces for joint operations and authorised military assistance within Marzena. With approximately 105,000 active personnel, it is the largest armed service. Its eleven manoeuvre brigades combine a limited heavy component with medium and light formations, supported by Army aviation, artillery, engineers, signals, intelligence and deployable logistics.

The Army's principal planning requirement is the defence of the northern corridor against a major conventional attack. Prepared positions, combined-arms brigades and concentrated supporting capabilities are intended to deny an opponent a rapid breakthrough. The Army also supplies expeditionary formations, reinforces other national theatres and sustains a growing commitment in the southern interior. These responsibilities draw on the same personnel, vehicles, helicopters and technical services.

The operational force is professional and normally deploys through brigade packages. A brigade headquarters receives its manoeuvre elements and supporting attachments as a coherent force, although not every constituent unit deploys on every occasion. Operational command passes through the FAM's joint theatre system; the Army recruits, trains, equips and sustains the formations assigned to it.

The institution combines substantial regimental continuity with a modern brigade organisation. Successive reforms favoured a direct brigade-and-battalion structure, but regiments retained responsibility for several permanent battalions, professional preparation and inherited colours. Their preservation shapes the present order of battle as much as equipment or geography. The resulting force is capable of sustained combined-arms activity, while its principal contemporary difficulty is maintaining preparation for continental war alongside southern and overseas commitments.

## Missions and employment

Continental defence determines the Army's heavy-force requirements, artillery preparation and mobilisation plans. The two mechanised brigades prepare for service under COI Nord, supported by medium formations and Army-level artillery and engineering units. Defensive planning concentrates on transport junctions, prepared sectors and routes through which reserves and supplies can move. Holding selected ground is intended to preserve national command and the ability to continue the campaign, rather than establish an equally dense military presence along every kilometre of frontier.

Southern missions place different demands on the service. Infantry, protected vehicles, engineers and aviation operate across greater distances and a thinner infrastructure network. Route protection, dispersed detachments and assistance to civilian-led security operations require sustained rotations. The Army's contribution to the [[Mission de Coopération Sécuritaire au Sahel]] adds overseas training, protection, reconnaissance and selected combat support. The airborne, marine and other light formations also provide forces for national reinforcement and the opening of a new theatre.

The Army maintains only a small permanent establishment in the Canary Islands. The Navy's coastal infantry regiment supplies the principal standing infantry presence there. Army personnel provide land-force liaison, reception and support for reinforcement; substantial Army combat formations normally arrive from the mainland when assigned. Naval coastal infantry and Army marine infantry are separate organisations, with different administrative parents despite overlapping qualifications.

A brigade package combines an established headquarters, a selected regimental or battalion grouping, organic support and Army or joint attachments. It may be smaller than the brigade's full peacetime establishment. This method preserves a headquarters that has trained with its principal units and can expand an operation without reconstructing command relationships. Individual battalions and specialist detachments still deploy, particularly for training and routine assistance, but they do so within a defined brigade or joint support arrangement.

## History

### Foundation and inherited regiments

The Army was formed from Marzenian personnel in French colonial service, locally raised formations and new republican recruits. French-trained officers provided much of the early staff competence. Existing regimental communities supplied instructors, clerks, specialists and recognised channels of command at a time when the Republic could not readily replace them with wholly new institutions.

The government retained selected infantry, cavalry and artillery lineages while transferring their service to the Republic. Names such as *tirailleurs*, *chasseurs*, *dragons* and *cuirassiers* survived as regimental designations. They did not preserve separate legal privileges or communal recruiting rights. Older titles often outlived the role that had produced them: a frontier regiment could serve away from a border, while a cavalry regiment could operate tanks or reconnaissance vehicles.

The founding force was smaller, less mechanised and more dependent on temporary groupings than its successor. Brigades coordinated infantry and attached support, but much professional identity remained at regimental level. Proposals to simplify the hierarchy encountered a practical objection as well as a historical one: regiments maintained training and personnel continuity across battalions whose readiness and deployment cycles differed. Later reforms retained the regimental institution while strengthening brigade authority over combined-arms preparation.

### Position 17 and the 1963 frontier war

*Main article: [[La Guerre des Frontières (1963)]].*

The 1963 war exposed deficiencies in warning, communications, ammunition and the coordination of arriving formations. CEMA Marc Delane established an improvised theatre headquarters under General Paul Azran. Azran concentrated the 5th Infantry Brigade and available artillery around the central road system while the 2nd Infantry Brigade assembled farther north. Recovering every lost post immediately was subordinated to stabilising the force and defending the principal junctions.

The fighting at Position 17 became the Army's central example of combined-arms defence under material constraint. Infantry held the ridge system, artillery concentrated limited ammunition and air deliveries restored critical supplies when ground access failed. The defence narrowly succeeded; it did not destroy the opposing force or end the campaign. A subsequent limited counteroffensive restored communications and recovered positions without extending the war into undisputed Algerian territory.

The 7th Light Infantry Battalion, repeatedly isolated southeast of Position 17, depended on air supply and considerable subordinate initiative. It became the 7th Parachute Regiment in 1965, and its lineage subsequently passed into the 7th Parachute Brigade. The original battalion had conducted no parachute assault. Its significance lay in remaining operational despite interrupted communications and ground supply, an experience later associated with airborne self-sufficiency.

The review also examined serious failures. Light armoured reconnaissance vehicles had been employed beyond their protection and terrain capabilities. An incorrectly relayed artillery reference caused Marzenian casualties during the battle, an incident acknowledged only after later archival disclosures. These cases entered professional teaching alongside the successful defence. The resulting reforms expanded artillery, engineering, communications, air-ground coordination and domestic maintenance capacity.

### Airborne opening in the Canaries

*Main article: [[Operation CEINTURE DE FER]].*

During the campaign of 14–24 October 1973, the Army's most distinctive contribution was the use of small airborne forces to establish access for larger follow-on elements. Selected troops and pathfinders from the 7th Parachute Brigade conducted the initial insertions. Additional personnel and supplies arrived after landing areas and airfields could be used. The operation did not involve dropping the entire brigade simultaneously.

The 6th Marine Infantry Brigade deployed task groups from military auxiliaries and adapted commercial shipping. Subsequent reinforcement depended on close sequencing between Army units, naval transport and aviation. Three Marzenian personnel were wounded during the campaign, with no fatalities recorded on either side. Spanish restraint and negotiated arrangements were integral to that outcome.

Army studies treated the campaign as evidence that a limited advance force could enable a larger deployment when political and operational conditions permitted. The more enduring institutional lesson concerned preparation: airborne opening, maritime movement and the reception of follow-on troops needed to be planned together. The low losses did not establish that lightly equipped infantry could routinely force entry against a fully defended coast.

### The War of the Sands and the Deux-Puits withdrawal

*Main article: [[La Guerre des Sables]].*

The War of the Sands, fought from 1978 to 1982, tested the Army's ability to sustain a prolonged conventional campaign. Artillery, repair, engineering and transport determined the endurance of formations. Prepared defences reduced the value of simple numerical comparisons, but successful local engagements did not compel the adversary to abandon the war. The Army experienced both more effective support than in 1963 and a much heavier cumulative demand on that support.

The withdrawal from the **Deux-Puits ridge**, conducted in November 1980, became a particular subject of regimental history. The name referred to a military map feature in the plateau corridor rather than a major settlement. The 5th Infantry Regiment had held an exposed section of the line whose communications became increasingly difficult to maintain. Colonel Lucien Darmont received authority to withdraw the formation to a shorter position supporting the rear road network.

The movement took place under continued pressure and intermittent communications failure. Some heavy stores and damaged vehicles were abandoned. The regiment nevertheless preserved the greater part of its battalions, weapons and supporting personnel and re-established a coherent defensive position. Its survival helped prevent a local contraction from becoming the disorganisation of the wider sector. The subsequent stabilisation depended on neighbouring formations and renewed support as well as the regiment's own conduct.

Adjudant Samir Bellane, a maintenance detachment leader, became associated with the episode for recovering vehicles carrying medical stores and communications equipment. Later accounts sometimes reduced the withdrawal to the actions of a few rear parties. Regimental records gave equal attention to transport allocation, repair work and the reception of exhausted units. The episode helped establish support personnel as participants in the formation's battle honour rather than an anonymous service behind it.

The Army commemorates Deux-Puits as an organised withdrawal that preserved combat power. It was a loss of ground, incurred casualties and left materiel behind; its military value lay in what remained available afterward. Staff teaching uses it to examine the point at which holding a position ceases to serve the defence of the larger front. This qualification became part of prepared-defence doctrine rather than an abandonment of it.

### Professionalisation and the brigade settlement

The post-war Army expanded its ability to coordinate artillery, aviation and reconnaissance. The return of tactical intelligence functions from DRET to military authority, through the development of CRMO, restored more responsive battlefield collection. Army units retained strong professional connections with the national intelligence system while acquiring clearer operational tasking relationships.

The permanent joint theatre headquarters established in the early 1990s gave the Army a stable framework for committing formations. Brigades became the principal combined-arms packages assigned to those headquarters. During the same decade, professional enlisted personnel and experienced NCOs replaced conscripts as the basis of operational units. Regiments gained more continuous training cycles and no longer regularly lost a large part of their trained manpower at the end of short compulsory service.

The reform settlement preserved regiments with multiple battalions inside brigades. Central proposals had favoured fewer command layers, but the retained regiments absorbed responsibilities that would otherwise have required brigade personnel and training staffs. Their headquarters were kept relatively small and were not intended to duplicate brigade operations centres. Operational groupings could still place a battalion under another regimental or brigade headquarters for a defined mission.

Later changes differentiated light formations without adding to the eleven-brigade manoeuvre establishment. The former 8th Light Intervention Brigade became the **8e Brigade d'infanterie**; the former 10th Rapid Reaction Brigade became the **10e Brigade aéromobile**. The mountain/desert brigade received the ordinal **13e**, ending its duplicate numbering with the 6th Marine Infantry Brigade. Renumbering did not extinguish its regimental lineages or distinctions.

The former 11th Artillery and 12th Long-Range Fires Brigade headquarters were replaced by a single artillery command. Their identities passed to regiments within a more compact organisation, while the rocket role acquired its own regiment. Recent personnel measures have favoured maintenance, movement, signals and training capacity over additional manoeuvre brigades.

### Sahel service and the growing domestic commitment

Since the establishment of the MCSS in 2007, Army deployments have combined partner training, protection, engineering, headquarters support and selected combat activity. The 8th Infantry Brigade provides an important source of sustained expeditionary rotations. Marine, airborne, mountain/desert and other formations contribute according to the mission, with aviation and logistics frequently proving more difficult to replace than infantry.

Experience has strengthened practical deployment preparation. Units train with their expected support, establish replacement arrangements and rehearse the transition from national movement to theatre sustainment. A brigade headquarters can coordinate dispersed battalions and detachments without requiring the full brigade to occupy one base. These improvements have not removed dependence on air transport, communications and the Juby support area.

The Foreign Legion battalion had its strongest recruiting period in the 1960s and 1970s. Recruitment subsequently slowed substantially, leaving a smaller flow of volunteers into a formation whose professional experience remained useful. It retained battalion scale and a strong Sahel association while becoming available for wider expeditionary service. Recruiting totals have not justified a new multi-battalion legion regiment.

The Interior Insurgency has generated an accumulating domestic commitment, particularly since 2022. Units provide protected movement, engineering, reconnaissance and support to civilian security institutions, while selected formations also conduct authorised combat operations. Repeated rotations consume preparation time and equipment life even when individual engagements are limited. Broad subordinate discretion, valued in conventional doctrine, has also created difficulties where tactical decisions have immediate consequences for civilian authority and the legitimacy of a security operation.

## Command and organisation

### Army administration and joint operational command

The **Chef d'état-major de l'Armée de Terre** heads the service from Lévane. The Army staff manages personnel, force preparation, equipment, resources and service administration. It combines these central functions with permanent commands for aviation, artillery, signals and training. Manoeuvre brigades, the two logistics brigades and specified independent units remain directly on the Army establishment.

There are no permanent divisions, army corps or territorial military districts. The service does not maintain a separate geographic operational chain parallel to the COIs. Garrison organisations support installations; joint headquarters command operations. A larger land component headquarters can be assembled for a campaign without acquiring permanent peacetime ownership of its constituent brigades.

Brigade commanders are responsible for collective preparation and the readiness of their assigned units. Regimental commanders maintain their battalions, training and personnel continuity within that framework. The Army chief provides formations to the CEMA, who assigns them to joint commands. The usual planning relationship shown in the order of battle is therefore distinct from permanent administrative subordination.

The *chasseurs* regiments listed with infantry battalions are infantry organisations; their title does not make them cavalry. The marine brigade's expeditionary establishment supports sustained service ashore as well as embarkation on BPCs.

The 11th and 12th regiments preserve the distinctions of the former artillery brigades. Rocket formations are grouped in the 14th; the transfer of brigade lineage does not imply that the 12th retained every former weapon role. The ballistic component consists of a limited munition option for compatible rocket launchers, using the appropriate launch-pod configuration. It is not a separate Army strategic missile regiment. Ownership, mission and release authority are all considered when assigning tasks. The CFM retains the national missile establishment and reserved strategic responsibilities.

Public organisational descriptions do not enumerate the Army-origin components inside CSOF. Their recruitment and support relationships are classified to a greater degree than the conventional ORBAT. Personnel transferred or seconded there are counted according to their establishment assignment and are not presented as an additional unlisted conventional regiment.

Aggregate active strength remains approximately 105,000; the upper limits of independently stated planning bands are not simultaneous funded headcounts.

## Doctrine and force preparation

Army doctrine applies the FAM principles of defence by denial, economy of forces and logistical flexibility to ground operations. Its central professional requirement is **combined-arms competence**: infantry, armour, artillery, engineers, reconnaissance and aviation must prepare together rather than rely on coordination improvised after deployment. Brigade packages provide the institutional basis for this cooperation.

Prepared defence is the principal continental method. Positions, communications, supply arrangements and routes for reinforcement are developed before a crisis where possible. Infantry and engineers make selected ground difficult to seize, while tanks and other mobile forces provide counterattack and reinforcement. Prepared defence does not require every unit to remain permanently in its first position. Commanders may reposition to preserve the larger defence, subject to their assigned objectives and the consequences for neighbouring formations.

The comparatively small and ageing tank establishment makes cooperation particularly important. Armour is expected to exploit preparation, infantry protection, reconnaissance and supporting fires. The Army does not treat tank crews' proficiency as a substitute for protection, serviceability or supply. Medium formations provide additional manoeuvre and protected infantry without claiming the same capacity to absorb concentrated fire as a modern heavy force.

Commanders normally state objectives, priorities and constraints while allowing subordinates considerable discretion over execution. This practice draws on the Army's early experience of interrupted communications and remains important during degraded SICO exercises. Regimental and battalion commanders are expected to continue pursuing the operation's purpose when detailed instructions are unavailable. Training therefore examines judgement and reporting as well as compliance with a sequence of orders.

The approach has proved more difficult in the insurgency environment. A local decision that appears militarily useful may affect a civilian investigation, the authority of another service or the acceptability of a wider operation. Existing reviews have identified cases of inconsistent interpretations of protection, detention support and the limits of a military task. Broad discretion remains doctrine, but the Army increasingly stresses explicit legal boundaries and the requirement to report changes in an operation's character. No separate internal-security command structure has replaced the joint and civilian arrangements.

Readiness is concentrated in elite and deployed formations. Other units retain personnel and equipment but may need additional collective preparation before receiving a demanding assignment. Brigade certification considers the package that will actually deploy, including communications, maintenance, medical support and attached aviation. A formation's nominal equipment holding does not certify the readiness of its support system.

Mobilisation is tested through regular limited recalls and equipment activations. Selected units call up reserve samples, inspect stored vehicles and rehearse movement and reception. These tests complement REMPART and service exercises without routinely mobilising the entire reserve. Findings are used to amend records, identify training needs and correct unusable allocations. Reserve formations requiring heavier equipment are not assumed to become ready merely because their personnel have reported.

## Personnel and ranks

### Professional service

The Army's approximately 105,000 active personnel include combat formations, aviation, supporting arms, schools, headquarters and the permanent cadres needed for mobilisation. The manoeuvre brigades account for roughly the mid-fifty-thousands at a typical funded establishment. The remainder provides the technical and administrative capacity needed to sustain them. Current force development gives particular weight to maintenance, movement, signals and instruction rather than increasing the brigade count.

Operationally assignable personnel are fewer than the active establishment. Training posts, longer maintenance cycles, medical limitations and preparation for later rotations all affect availability. The Army can generate substantial brigade packages, but committing every unit at once would remove its replacement and training capacity. Planning therefore distinguishes an authorised post, a filled post and a person available for the particular operation.

The limited four-month national-service programme is filled by volunteers in practice. It provides basic military experience, training credit and routes into further service. Participants are not counted as an automatically qualified pool for complex combat tasks. Professional entry requires the relevant training and assessment, with prior service credited where the individual has already met the required standard.

### Rank reform

The Army adopted a simplified French-derived hierarchy during the personnel reforms accompanying SICO's rollout in the late 2010s. The review addressed differences between recorded grade, local title and actual appointment that had complicated common personnel and readiness reporting. Legislative and service instruments changed the rank structure; SICO implemented the resulting common records.

The reform removed *soldat de première classe* and *caporal-chef* as separate grades and consolidated the older NCO ladder into **sergent**, **adjudant** and **major**. Existing service, pay protection and professional qualifications were handled through transition arrangements rather than erased with the titles. Distinctions between experience levels can still be recorded without creating additional substantive ranks.

Rank names are uniform across the Army. Cavalry, artillery and infantry retain different unit terminology, but do not use separate equivalent rank titles. A *major* is a senior NCO, distinct from a commissioned field officer. *Aspirant* denotes officer-candidate status outside the permanent commissioned hierarchy.

### Training and advancement

Professional enlisted preparation uses a hybrid system. Common basic instruction and occupational training normally take about six months, followed by substantial preparation within the receiving formation. Technical trades can require longer before independent qualification. The initial period prepares a soldier to join a unit; it does not confer every collective, environmental or deployment qualification that the unit may require.

NCO development is primarily internal. Experienced enlisted personnel are selected for formal instruction and assessed leadership appointments. The Army values practical familiarity with vehicles, weapons, maintenance and soldiers' work alongside classroom preparation. Senior NCOs retain instructional and technical authority even when posted outside a manoeuvre unit.

Combat-command officers normally begin at the Académie militaire de Lévane. Technical officers initially follow the École technique militaire route, with military and engineering instruction suited to their future employment. Both paths lead into the same commissioned rank structure. Later command eligibility depends on the required courses and experience rather than the original institution alone. Selected personnel can also advance from enlisted or NCO service into officer preparation.

Specialist qualifications combine central standards with instruction by operational formations. The airborne and marine communities provide practical instructors; the mountain/desert brigade contributes environmental expertise; engineers, aviation and signals organisations certify their own technical skills under common Army requirements. Qualifications have renewal conditions where safety or operational proficiency requires them. A historic unit title does not automatically qualify every member for every specialist task.

Reservists normally undertake short annual training periods, supplemented by the limited mobilisation tests described above. Active formations identify the tasks for which individual reservists remain current. More demanding combat or technical appointments may require additional preparation before assignment. Senior officers attend joint education through ESGI, which complements rather than replaces Army combined-arms instruction.

## Equipment and modernisation

### Armour and protected mobility

The **Leopard 1A5-MZ** is the Army's main battle tank. The fleet derives from successive foreign acquisitions and national refurbishment, with Marzenian communications, selected protection and support modifications. The modernisation standard preserves useful firepower and familiarity but does not remove the underlying limits of an older tank design.

The two mechanised brigades hold the principal operational tank allocation. Their four tank groups together account for approximately 168 authorised vehicles, within an active-assignment band of 160–180. Other hulls support training, stored reserves, maintenance rotation and parts recovery. Not every stored vehicle is expected to return to service economically.

VBCI and VAB fleets remain central to infantry mobility. Lighter formations use a protected core alongside companies equipped for movement in lighter vehicles. National rebuilding keeps older vehicles useful, but differing protection, communications and mechanical standards complicate allocation. The Army continues these programmes while examining alternatives rather than assuming that the entire fleet can be replaced in one procurement cycle.

### Artillery, air defence and unmanned systems

Tube artillery remains organic to every manoeuvre brigade. The standard heavy and medium establishments provide groups of twelve guns; the six lighter brigades provide groups of eight. The two Army-level tube-artillery regiments provide additional batteries, while actual deployed packages can be smaller or reinforced. Mortars and other infantry support weapons are accounted for separately.

The rocket regiment supplies heavy rocket artillery and guided multiple-launch capability. A small tactical ballistic munition holding broadens the compatible launchers' mission options. It does not change the regiment into an independent strategic force, and the availability of a compatible launcher does not by itself confer release authority. Exact munition holdings remain restricted.

Short-range air defence is organised through brigade-level elements. The Army is introducing **self-propelled anti-aircraft guns**, intended to improve protection against drones and other low-altitude threats while accompanying mobile forces. Trials, crew preparation and integration with the recognised air picture are still under way. Existing short-range weapons remain in service; the new vehicles are not yet a complete Army-wide capability or a substitute for the AAE's national air-defence system.

Established tactical-UAS companies serve the **1st and 2nd Mechanised, 3rd and 5th Motorised, 6th Marine, 7th Parachute and 8th Infantry Brigades**. The other four manoeuvre brigades receive attachments when available. Expansion is constrained by trained operators, secure links, maintenance and the capacity to interpret collected information, rather than airframe procurement alone.

Loitering munitions remain an experimental capability in early operational use. Reconnaissance and artillery organisations employ different systems according to their tasks; CSOF conducts its own restricted activity outside the conventional Army establishment. Experience in the Sahel informs evaluation, but the Army has not established universal infantry distribution or treated trials as evidence of a mature service-wide capability. Larger MALE aircraft remain operated by the Air and Space Force.

### Infantry weapons

The **FAMAS family** remains the principal service-rifle family, acquired through imports and licensed Marzenian production. Local manufacture and refurbishment support existing stocks without making each unit's rifle a separate sovereign design. Rifles are supplemented by general-purpose machine guns, heavy machine guns, precision weapons, infantry mortars and portable anti-armour systems.

Anti-armour provision is layered. Ordinary infantry elements carry simpler weapons suited to their role, while specialist teams and supporting units hold more demanding guided systems. This supplements the limited tank establishment without eliminating the need for protected manoeuvre, reconnaissance and supporting fires. Ammunition, training and the readiness of operators receive the same attention as launcher numbers.

### Aviation equipment

Army aviation retains a diverse fleet of attack, light reconnaissance, medium utility and heavy transport helicopters. Its size reflects the need to connect dispersed ground forces and support formations operating beyond convenient road access. Multiple generations and support arrangements impose a considerable burden on technicians and maintenance capacity.

### Replacement studies and the “-MZ” problem

A tank replacement programme is in the study and trials phase. Imported acquisition, licensed assembly and a more substantial national industrial contribution remain under consideration. Requirements seek improved protection and supportability together with maintenance authority and national integration. No preferred concept is treated as an already contracted operational fleet.

Other studies address protected mobility, artillery support, helicopters and mobile air defence. Their maturity differs, but most major replacement decisions remain open. The Army's near-term capacity therefore depends primarily on existing equipment and improved sustainment, not the assumed arrival of every proposed programme.

The wider FAM discussion of the **“-MZ curse”** applies directly to the Army. National modifications can preserve operational control and extend service life, while increasing the number of standards that workshops and supply systems must support. The service has benefited from substantial domestic refurbishment. Its procurement problem concerns how to retain those benefits without repeating expensive variation across every replacement family.

## Regimental traditions and remembrance

Regimental colours and battle honours remain central to Army identity. Distinctions are awarded for identifiable actions rather than automatically for presence in a campaign. The honour attaches to the recognised formation and passes through a documented lineage when the unit is reorganised. Changing a brigade's number does not automatically transfer every distinction held by its subordinate regiments to the new brigade headquarters.

The 7th Parachute community commemorates the isolated battalion of 1963 and the airborne opening of 1973 as different achievements. The 5th Infantry Regiment associates Position 17 with holding essential ground and Deux-Puits with preserving the formation when withdrawal became necessary. These traditions supply contrasting examples within the same broader doctrine of national defence.

Ceremonies retain the shared FAM oath to the Republic, its Constitution and lawful military duty. The 2 May founding observances include remembrance of the dead, with regiments conducting their own readings and presentation of colours. Individual accounts, including those of support personnel, are used in instruction and remembrance without replacing the unit's collective record.

Formal presentation is generally restrained. Older regimental names, colours and selected ceremonial distinctions coexist with common rank titles and republican insignia. The Army treats continuity as a means of preserving professional obligations and memory through changes in equipment and organisation.

## Contemporary commitments and assessment

The Army can generate credible brigade packages, prepare substantial ground defences and support limited overseas commitments. Its strongest formations combine experienced personnel with established working relationships across combat and supporting arms. Recent organisational changes strengthen the logistics, communications and technical capacity on which these formations depend.

The central unresolved issue is the competition between missions. Southern and overseas rotations consume the same aviation hours, instructors, maintenance capacity and collective preparation periods required for major-war readiness. A unit may return with valuable operational experience while still needing substantial retraining for a different kind of campaign. Supporting organisations can remain heavily committed even when the infantry rotation has changed.

The Republic continues to require a continental shield, a deployable regional force and military assistance during domestic emergencies. The eleven-brigade establishment is intended to preserve that breadth without creating separate armies for each task. Continuing tank studies, uneven drone provision, the transition in mobile air defence and a diverse aviation fleet show the practical limits within which that ambition operates. Successive force allocations and budgets continue to reconcile those demands without abandoning the Army's primary responsibility for continental defence.
